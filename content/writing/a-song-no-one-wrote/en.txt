The biggest lie she had ever been told wasn't the young startup founder, a college dropout, holding forth about how some monkey NFT counted as "real art." It wasn't the endless half-finished sweet talk of men and women who didn't mean a word of it. It wasn't her father, half drunk on his knees in front of a blank canvas with a Malbec in one hand and a palette in the other, saying slowly, "How do you get inspired without a drink?" The biggest lie was finding out that the song she'd been in love with lately had been put together by an AI. She'd just landed at Beijing Capital Airport and was waiting at baggage claim, the track playing in her earbuds: "Same Girl, Different Wig." She was early. The carousel went round empty, and the dark mouth it came out of gaped open like a jaw, a long black forked tongue sliding out. She took out her phone to check the way to her hotel and saw that the artist suddenly had a bio: "Contemporary electronic AI musician, since 2021."

The track had no lyrics, just a simple four-on-the-floor beat trading off with a bass line. Technically, it was the kind of thing a few lines of code could produce. But she'd always felt it was different, though she could never say how. Honestly, it was just the kind of music a jazz lounge plays. It made her think of thick carpet soaking up most of the tobacco smoke, high-backed sofas in old-fashioned patterns, a dusk outside the window that was yellowing but not yet dark, dust floating in slanting evening light. You needed at least one glass of cabernet in you before you could really hear it. However many glasses of cabernet she'd had in New York, that was how many times she'd played it.

When she was on her own, she always picked one song to put on repeat, so that stretch of time alone would be stamped with a song of its own. It was like scoring the film of her own life, carefully choosing music to fit the scene. The scene was where her inner world met her sense of the outer one, a balance between what a place actually looked like and what she was there for. So it wasn't really about fitting the scene. It was about fitting who she hoped to be in it, the moment she walked in.

Say you're walking by the river on a summer evening. The black sky is high and far, lights tremble on the water, there are hardly any people around. Objectively the mood is loose and easy, but if you've just had your heart broken, a breezy bossa nova is obviously wrong; some wrenching, howling love song, or a self-pitying murmur, will say it better.

Say you're browsing boutiques in SoHo on a sunny day, with a hundred steps up a slope like a cliff face between one shop and the next, and the heat at 40 degrees has worn you out. Metal or rap ought to fire you up to buy everything in sight. But if what you're really there for is watching the strangers who brush past and the shop owners with their big personalities, then deep house, with its crisp beat and its cryptic lyrics, is probably the better choice.

Or say it's a winter morning and you're winding through the hutongs looking for a teahouse. Old people sit in their doorways sizing up everyone who passes, a mother up early is buying breakfast at the stall, kids in school uniforms drag themselves off to class, and some cheerful folk song with that street-life feel seems about right. But if you've only come home looking for the teahouse you remember from childhood, wanting to be shut away from all the noise and quietly remember, then some trying-too-hard pop song with tacky lyrics, one that'll be forgotten in a month, seems better at stealing you out of where you are.

What a song holds for someone, the feelings and memories of a stretch of time, is completely subjective, and all of this was, of course, just her own view. You're free to think that every one of those scenes calls for Dvořák, or Janáček, or Tengger, or Wang Feng. Even if your taste is nothing like anyone else's and you can't explain why, that's fine. She had always believed that this kind of feeling, the kind logic can't account for, wasn't some spell cast by a creator. It was proof that people have agency. It was something you're born with that your life also shapes, part of what makes each life its own, part of free will. Or to put it another way: however vast and tangled fate might be, however it swept you along like a flood, she could still decide what music played under this one brief, unimportant scene of her life.

So when she found out the track was made by an AI, she felt sharply, furiously betrayed. Not that she listened to music as a connoisseur, needing to know the artist's era and background and genre. Whether the track had been coded, or dashed off by Bach on a whim, or recorded on a phone by some gifted kid after school and posted to Douyin, it didn't change in the slightest how she'd felt the first time she heard it. She'd done what she always did: arrived in a strange city alone, shuffled her playlist until something fit the scene, and made it the theme for that stretch of time. This track happened to fit, happened to match who she hoped to be in those days after landing in New York. Shouldn't that have been enough?

But she was furious, the way you are when you've been insulted. She'd thought she had chosen this beat, and that the reason she never tired of it might be that its rise and fall and its easy rhythm had unlocked some buried memory, or that some splinter of its maker's soul was like hers, or simply that some tangle of variables she couldn't name had made it fill in exactly what was missing from her days in New York. Whatever the reason, she had taken one thing for granted: that the song's particularity, its subjectivity, served her free will. That she had chosen the song, and not the other way around.

Now it turned out the track was nothing but a string of cold zeros and ones. A neural network had made it and a recommendation algorithm had delivered it to her ears. It came into the world for no reason and led to nothing. It wasn't written by a man for someone he'd loved and lost, or by a long-haired girl who loved the drums in a burst of inspiration at midnight, or by a pianist past fifty who'd gone back to his hometown and been moved. It took less than a second to make, and the people who wrote the code didn't even know it existed. Its name meant nothing. Its rise and fall meant nothing. However special you thought it was, it was only something a machine learning model had learned to produce.

Her black suitcase finally came round. She grabbed it and hurried toward the exit. At customs a middle-aged man in uniform stood holding a sign, wearing a mechanical professional smile, telling every passing traveler, day after day, year after year: nothing to declare, to the left; goods to declare, to the right. Early spring in Beijing had no wind; the air was cold and clean, the sky high and blue. "Same Girl, Different Wig" was still looping in her ears.

She stood on the curb watching the crowds slowly absorbed by the taxis and airport buses. Then she heard something, and suddenly understood why the song had always felt different. She pulled out her phone and dragged the track back five seconds. At 3:28 there was a woman's sigh. It was so soft you could almost miss it, nearly drowned in the tail of a drumbeat. But once you heard it, you couldn't stop hearing it. It felt strangely heavy, as if years of pent-up sorrow were being let out all at once. Had the sigh always been there? Why would there be a sigh there? she wondered, stunned. And then a huge despair and grief took hold of her. The sigh was only one more output of all the variables in the model. What she'd taken for the unexpected, the unique, could be explained away by the variables of some enormous model, a sleight of hand the AI had pulled on her. Whether the sigh was there or not, and why, it couldn't change the fact that this was a song no one wrote. None of it meant anything.

Her car arrived. She put her bag in the trunk and got into the seat behind the driver, which people said was the safest. She could have sat up front. She could have not got in at all, could have stayed at the airport until dark, could have turned off the song. She seemed to have endless choices, seemed to be the one making every decision, large and small, that would decide where her life went. But she was like the man at customs, like everyone in the world, just a stone that had been thrown. It looked as if she chose her own path, as if she could cross times and places, but in the end her path was only another perfect parabola under gravity. When it came down to it, she was just another same girl in a different wig, another song no one wrote.
