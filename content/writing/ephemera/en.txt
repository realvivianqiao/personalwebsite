It was a warm summer evening, and he was sitting on a bench by the river watching the sun go down. The sunset was gorgeous and yet the same as every other: such a dangerous, seductive combination. The sameness makes you weary and makes you plan your escape; the gorgeousness makes you stop, and look, and hesitate. His wife had just texted that she and their son would be back from Rome tomorrow. She complained that she had taken him to so many galleries and museums, with private expert guides the whole way, "because a sense of art has to be cultivated young," but their son was listless and hadn't taken in a thing. Seeing the message, he realized with a start that it was already the end of August. Another summer leaving. He couldn't remember how many summers he had spent in New York.

Since he had become an adult, every milestone in his life had avoided summer without exception. Winter, on the other hand, had thoughtfully supplied everything a stable life required. The wedding was in the depths of winter, his son was born at the end of winter, and news of every promotion always came just before Christmas. And still he looked forward to summer. Maybe because the people of this city love summer beyond all reason, since the winters are so long and gloomy that summer feels precious. Or maybe because his wife always took their son out of New York for a long summer vacation, while he, unable to get away from work, stayed behind alone. No one asked about his hangovers, coming home late needed no explanation, and he could walk by the river as he pleased. These brief, free summers were the only way out of his respectable, unchanging life.

Since childhood he'd had a remarkable memory. Convoluted statutes, the tongue-twisting names of German clients, long monologues from his favorite plays, the scores of the seasons his team won, he could remember all of it word for word. But many of his memories of summer, and only those, were hazy. He could still clearly remember many summer moments, but he had trouble placing them right away, where they belonged and when, and how they connected. He remembered leaning over the edge of a rooftop terrace seventy-two floors up, looking down at the city's traffic, glad to be away for a moment from the flattery and clinking glasses behind him, the smoke ring he blew out swallowed by the damp air after rain before it had time to fade. He remembered a red peacock tattoo on a girl's arm, glimpsed through the beat of the music and the flicker of the lights; for a long time afterward he wondered whether the peacock really was red, or had been dyed by the red lasers. He remembered stumbling out of a dim, smoky basement bar, and on the wall of the abandoned warehouse across the street a graffiti pig with a cigarette in its mouth, the wallpaper at the tip of the cigarette peeling and curling up so it looked like ash about to drop to the ground. And he clearly remembered a smell. A smell like blooming lilacs and crumbled tobacco sealed for years in a wooden box and then opened, flowers and sandalwood and tobacco and dust all mixed together, warm and old.

When people remember, they always follow a logic of their own, and for him, his memories always started from smell. That warm, old smell belonged to the girl with the peacock tattoo. When he stumbled out of the basement bar, she was standing beside the abandoned warehouse, smoking. And the reason he had gone to that bar at all was that the cocktail party on the seventy-second-floor terrace had ended early. The first time they spoke, the girl started it. "Hey, why'd you wear a suit to a place like this?" Before he could answer, she wrinkled her nose and added, "Careful you don't drink so much you lose your diamond ring." He quickly looked down at the ring on his finger and touched it, uneasy. The girl with the peacock tattoo was called Nae. She went to school downtown and studied music production. The peacock tattoo really was red, one of many tattoos on her body, and that summer she dyed her hair from pink to blond, and then from blond to a fiery red.

He spent most of his summers alone, but that summer, and only that one, he spent a lot of time with Nae and her friends. She did guest DJ sets at a few clubs in Bushwick and invited him to come. After her sets she introduced him to her boyfriend and her friends. They were all DJs, or photographers, or models, and every one of them walked with a floating lightness somewhere between fainting and grace. Nae always introduced him the same way: "This is my brother from another mother, and another father. Hotshot lawyer, fights for the rights of the capitalist sharks." He would laugh awkwardly and reach out by habit to shake hands and take a business card, then realize it was wrong and pull his hand back. She always wore strange makeup, eyeshadow that either echoed her hair color or clashed with it, glittering gems stuck on her face, at least five rings on her hands, and she talked with an irony that never quite tipped into offending anyone. Nae's boyfriend had hair longer than hers, a long dragon tattoo coiled around his neck, black nail polish, and a loose, drifting smile. And he always wore a shirt and slacks, his skin pale and clean, and looked completely out of place among them.

They lived close to each other, so after her sets he drove her home. The car flew across the Brooklyn Bridge, and Nae always told him to go faster. "If you drive a little faster, maybe we can break free of gravity and fly straight to the moon." He said that was ridiculous, but quietly pressed the gas, secretly half hoping that when the car reached the top of the bridge it really would break free. Nae had grown up with her father in Cyprus. He was an architect of some local renown, and he had bought her an apartment by Central Park. "Too far from school and from Brooklyn," Nae said, pouting. "I'm in Brooklyn most of the time. Manhattan belongs to you elite types." She had loved painting since she was little and always wanted to be an oil painter, but after she came to New York she fell in love with electronic music. "I think music is the thing that brings people together most. Especially techno. You don't even need lyrics, just the beat and the rhythm." As she talked she connected to the car's Bluetooth and played tracks she had recorded herself. Nae had only fifty listeners on Spotify, and he became her fifty-first. When Nae asked about him, he only told her the basics. His wife was his college classmate; they had dated five years and been married seven; their son had just turned five; his wife and son were in Malta right now. He had been at the same law firm since he graduated. Work was busy, there was plenty of politics, but luckily he was senior enough and good enough. Nae's eyes went wide in disbelief. "You're a model citizen. My parents weren't married as long as you two dated." He shrugged. "My life's very comfortable. You're still too young. Give it a few years and you'll understand." Nae leaned back in the passenger seat laughing so hard she couldn't breathe. "Trust me, in twenty years I still won't understand."

He mentioned Nae once on the phone with his wife, and his wife was full of suspicion. He explained again and again that they were just friends across a big age gap, that he had even met Nae's boyfriend, but his wife was still unhappy. In the end he changed the subject and never mentioned her to his wife again. That summer ended quickly. Nae was going to Florence with her boyfriend for an exchange semester in the fall, and she said a hurried goodbye and left. His wife and son came home. Their son was about to start elementary school; they had gone to enormous lengths to get him into the best private school on the Upper East Side. Work got busier, and life slowly went back to the plain shuttle between two points. At the end of that year he was promoted again, climbing higher and higher on the corporate ladder. His wife was delighted. At gatherings of the other wives she always brought it up as if in passing, then looked around with satisfaction, catching the envy in everyone's eyes.

"Which summer was that?" The sky had darkened, and the last of the setting sun was slowly drowning in the water. "If I was promoted at the end of that year, it must have been three years ago," he murmured to himself. After Nae came back to New York she got in touch now and then, but he was tied up with work and errands and was slow to reply. Then Nae graduated, and after graduation she went off to Berlin to chase her DJ dream, and spent her summers in Ibiza or the south of France.

But in every summer after that, he thought of Nae, even though they never spent another summer together, never even saw each other again. He had told Nae once that he thought there were two kinds of people in this world: those who know their mission in life from the day they're born, and those who spend their whole lives scraping by and never understand what they're after. Nae didn't agree. She said there were only free people and unfree people in this world. Free people aren't bound by anything, not even by what they're after or by a mission. Unfree people are bound by everything. Society's rules, other people's eyes, even a tiny tattoo, any of it can bind the way they look at the world.

He stared blankly at the water. The sky had gone completely dark, and the breeze on his face had a slight chill. There was a little Brooklyn Sour left in his glass, and he tipped his head back and drank the last of it. He knew that after tomorrow he couldn't smoke anymore; he couldn't let his family breathe secondhand smoke. He would have to drink less, too; it was bad for him, after all. He would have to work harder. His wife wanted another child, and the financial load would certainly get heavier.

He knew that after tomorrow, summer would be over again, and he would go back to his real life once more. He would put Nae out of his mind once more and bury himself in family errands and work. He would be happy and content with the exquisite meals his wife cooked, proud and glad as his son grew, and he would let out a long breath when he was promoted or made partner. He would forget his summer happiness and his summer pain. Like a mayfly in this world, he consoled himself, who is ever truly free?
