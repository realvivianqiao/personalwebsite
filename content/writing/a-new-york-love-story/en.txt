He hadn't properly thought about her in years, and then he thought of her at his wedding, of all places. The reason was absurd. One of his wife's friends had had far too much to drink and got into an argument with the bartender. "What do you mean there's no white wine? You just twist the cap! It's the easiest wine there is, how can you not have it?" He was the groom, the center of everything, surrounded by friends and family, and those words floated over from behind him, through all the deafening congratulations and good wishes, and reached his ear. And at exactly the wrong moment it dawned on him: to her, he had been nothing more than that. Convenient, cheap, just presentable enough. A white wine sold in every corner deli in New York, the kind you open with one twist.

The place he first met her was a classic: Central Park, by the statue where Fifty-Ninth Street meets Fifth Avenue.

The first time he came to Central Park was the winter he was fourteen, when his parents brought him to New York on vacation. As for why it had to be winter, the flights were cheaper, naturally. But on the way home they were caught in a blizzard, and their flight was delayed a whole day and night. His mother argued with United until they gave in and put the family up in a hotel near Central Park. Next to the World Trade Center, Wall Street, the Empire State Building and the rest, Central Park hadn't even been on his parents' itinerary. They grumbled under their breath. It's just a big park, California has them everywhere. A hotel near Wall Street would have been so much better!

Wall Street had been the first stop on that trip. They got off the plane and didn't even drop their bags before his father took him to touch the bronze bull. His father watched the men in sharp suits hurrying by and the long black cars with naked envy, squeezed his hand, and said, "We crossed an ocean and went through hell to immigrate here so you could have a better life. If you don't make something of yourself, you'll have let us down!"

When it came to defining what it meant to make something of yourself, his father was far ahead of the people around him. His father had graduated from Tsinghua years ago, in physics, too smart for his own good. He brought his mother, who was beautiful but hadn't had much schooling, to America full of confidence, to do a PhD in theoretical physics at a not particularly good school in California. Meanwhile, to keep the family fed, his mother worked at a local Chinese restaurant. Every so often some man would whistle at her and ask for her number, and she'd roll her eyes at him. My husband is going to be an American professor! How could the likes of you compare! But it took his father two years to realize, belatedly, that he simply wasn't cut out for research. He scraped through his degree and switched to computers. Google didn't exist yet, and the World Wide Web was still a novelty. His father started as a programmer at a startup, sold his stock after a few years and jumped to a bigger company. Today that stock would be worth an astonishing sum; his mother still sighs about it every time it comes up, even though back then she probably didn't know what a stock was. Still, the company he jumped to did well. He didn't get much equity, but he kept at it, steady as anything, and even though his English was poor and he wasn't good with people, hard work got him all the way to engineering manager, with a team of twenty-some under him. But his father felt that wasn't nearly enough. To really make something of yourself, you couldn't keep hunched behind a computer typing code. You had to go into investment banking, into finance, and boss around those swaggering Americans!

He thought it over and decided his father said this for two reasons. First, his father's English really was poor, and he was small and slight, with round glasses as thick as the bottoms of bottles. In his early years in America he had been on the receiving end of plenty of looks and plenty of hardship. When Americans gave him grief (usually a strange smile from a supermarket cashier or a mailman), he could never think of a single comeback, so he came home and lectured his son instead. Second, the senior student his father had most looked up to during his PhD was on Wall Street. That man's English was excellent, and he was tall and handsome. He'd left his PhD halfway through for Lehman Brothers, and after Lehman went under he jumped to Barclays, shuttling between New York and London, a very big title. "Married a blonde, blue-eyed white wife, has a whole team of Americans under him, a glorious life." Those were his father's exact words.

He certainly hadn't grown up in a family of great wealth, but as an only child he'd never wanted for food or clothes. Still, the old-generation thinking his parents had brought from China hadn't changed one bit. Money is for spending where it counts, and not a cent more than necessary! So at fourteen, the horse carriages in Central Park were out of the question, resort hotels with spa pools and breakfast were out of the question, and Michelin restaurants were absolutely, completely out of the question. But anything to do with his dream of making something of himself, not a single coin could be spared. The best prep school, philosophy and history summer programs in Europe, the very top business school, golf, skiing, piano, violin, handmade Italian suits and shoes. Nothing could be left out.

So when he graduated from Wharton and got an offer from Goldman Sachs's TMT group in New York, he wasn't entirely sure whether "living somewhere nice" had anything to do with making something of himself, and he called his parents specially to ask. "What kind of apartment should I rent?" The conclusion they reached: as long as it's safe, the cheaper the better. Start investing early, that's the smart thing! So he and two friends found an old prewar walk-up, its windows long and narrow and facing north. In the daytime, without the lights on, the place was like a dark nest built to some termite master plan. The three of them shared one bathroom. There was no dishwasher, and for laundry you had to go to the laundromat across the street. But after all, as his mother said, apart from you and your two roommates, who's going to see it?

What his mother didn't know, of course, was that when he brought girls home, the girls would see it. At first he was a little embarrassed, but then he found they didn't care at all, and after that he held nothing back. He'd been in New York for two years before he met her, and in that time he'd brought home at least fifty girls. Some he met at bars and clubs, some at company events, some at parties thrown by friends of friends, and now and then he used the dating apps.

He was tall to begin with, and his features were regular enough. He'd played football in high school and spent three years on the crew team in college, so he was very well built, which was rare among Asian men. His looks alone were enough for plenty of women to like him, and then you saw the school and the job: a young man of real promise! There were so many women who wanted a serious relationship with him, even marriage, before they'd even met. Who would care where he lived? Even though at the time he had no thought of marriage whatsoever, and even less of a relationship. A man as outstanding as him, one in ten thousand, how could he be tied down by one woman? Not that he ever said so to the girls he dated. They all thought he was a warm, sunny, devoted man with a bright future, one in ten thousand. Half of that was because he was a smooth talker, and the other half was probably because they wanted to think so. Women, after all, what do they fall in love with but an idea?

He was very sure of himself with the women of New York. But when he met her, he lost. They met on that dating app. They matched, chatted a little, and he asked her out. There was no special reason. He was very busy at work then, his life was short on women, and she seemed young and pretty and naive. He figured one or two dates and he could bring her home. That was all.

He still remembers the first time he saw her, in summer, in June. He'd changed subway lines twice and dodged three homeless men and arrived panting and drenched in sweat. And she came strolling over from a luxury building less than five minutes' walk away, without a drop of sweat on her. She greeted him with a dazzling smile. "Hi, hello, I'm Nae."

To this day he thinks Nae was beautiful, and not the way his wife is beautiful. His wife has to put on a custom dress from her favorite designer, push her breasts into the right shape, spend an hour on makeup, curl her hair into exactly the right wave and put on a gracious, attentive smile before he thinks she's beautiful. But that girl wore a white sundress, a canvas tote and big sunglasses, and he thought she was stunning. Even in New York, a city full of beautiful women, he'd have given her at least an eight.

On that first date they took the horse carriage, because Nae thought it would be fun. She studied literature at Columbia and was half Korean, half American. He assumed her mother was Korean and her father American; later he found out it was the other way around. She'd grown up moving between Korea, the US and England, and said she was an old soul. She liked jazz and old movies, and lately she'd been obsessed with documentaries about plane crashes. When the carriage stopped at a crowded corner, she eagerly pulled out her phone. She had just downloaded a magnetic field detector and wanted to see if there were any strong magnetic fields around.

Remembering that her father was Asian too, he told her about how harsh his parents were, and the jokes Asian kids tell each other about being the model student, figuring they probably had something in common. But she was the youngest daughter, and her parents had never put many expectations or much pressure on her. Remembering that she'd gone to high school in London, he mentioned that he'd spent a semester abroad at Oxford and gone down to Soho every weekend, and had been to every club there. She agreed, politely, that the music was pretty good. Remembering that she minored in art history, he told her about the Yayoi Kusama show he'd just seen in New York (no need to say which girl he went with; a hint was enough to show how irresistible he was). Pretty dimples bloomed in her cheeks, and she asked who his favorite artist was. Full of confidence, he said if it wasn't David Hockney it had to be Andy Warhol, and added a few lines he'd picked up from a museum audio guide. She only smiled and said nothing.

He thought he'd cracked the secret of New York women. What kind of woman she was, what strategy to use, he could tell at a glance. The insecure ones got three texts a day. The literary ones got long speeches about Goethe and Murakami. The practical, worldly ones got a mention of his degrees and his job and his weekend golf with colleagues at the country club. The simple ones got told she was the most beautiful woman he'd ever seen. He had a counter for every move, it never failed, and nine times out of ten he was the one steering the conversation.

But that day, sitting in that horribly bumpy carriage, and so many times afterward with Nae, his brain went on strike. He simply couldn't crack her. Nothing she said gave him anything to measure her against, and he was truly, deeply curious about what kind of person she really was.

Her apartment was spacious and beautiful, with huge floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the whole of Central Park and big, beautiful paintings on the walls. But in the short time they were together, while he held forth about his views on contemporary art and went on and on about who he'd run into at the country club, she only smiled and agreed now and then. She couldn't be bothered to give her own opinion. She couldn't be bothered to mention that the paintings were her mother's, or that the whole building was in her father's name. She couldn't be bothered to ask about or wonder about anything to do with him. Whether his parents made him learn violin or piano, whether he belonged to the country club, whether he liked swimming or tennis better, what his childhood dream was, what his childhood wound was, how many girlfriends he'd had. Even the most basic things, who he really was, what he really thought, what he was really after, none of it interested her much. She only asked, as a matter of routine, how his week had been, whether work was busy. After all, all she needed was someone, a man she thought was decent enough, presentable enough, to go with her to the designer shops in SoHo, try the new restaurants in Brooklyn, see the latest show at a friend's gallery. Or just to sit on the soft Minotti sofa, the two of them tipsy, aimlessly watching why Air France Flight 447 went down, listening to Billie Holiday, watching London stand-up from the nineties. That was enough.

They lasted less than six months. In the end it was Nae who broke it off. He came over as usual, and she opened a bottle of sauvignon blanc he'd bought at the deli nearby the last time he was there. The wine was called Oyster Bay. Its screw cap was the grey-blue of an overcast sky, and it came off with one light turn. The dark green glass bottle looked rather classy, but it actually cost less than thirteen dollars. Excellent value.

Sipping that cloyingly sweet wine, she told him, lightly, that she'd decided to move to London after graduation and work for a while at a gallery run by a friend of her mother's, so after that they probably wouldn't see each other. He could transfer to the London office. He even told her that. But she shook her head and only repeated, "After that we probably won't see each other." She changed her number, she never posted on social media, and he never saw Nae again.

He dated plenty of women after that. But for a long time, every woman he met, he couldn't help thinking of Nae. All the women, eyes thick with mascara blinking and blinking, soft lips opening and closing, slender waists swaying this way and that, were exactly alike. They talked about the same things, thought the same things, and mostly wanted the same things. Only Nae, cut off abruptly in his memory, seemed different.

Back then he knew, with total certainty, why she didn't want to stay with him. It was only because he wasn't successful enough, couldn't reach the people in her circle! Just as his mother, who understood how the world worked, sighed when she heard: "Our two families were never a match. That would have been marrying up!"

His wife now was also, as his mother put it, marrying up. His wife was honestly not beautiful, but her father founded a private equity firm, and her grandfather was a well-known name in New York politics. She met him at an alumni event and was very interested in him; when he heard her last name, he became a hundred times more enthusiastic. Then came dating, the proposal, and now, after overcoming every obstacle, the wedding. He had always worked hard, and now he was a tiger with wings. Still young, he had a name; there wasn't an Asian person in finance who didn't know who he was.

He already had everything he'd wanted when he met Nae. Status, money, making something of himself by his parents' simple definition, he had all of it. And yet, out of nowhere, he thought of Nae. He slipped away from the crowd, took out his phone and searched her name, and went through several pages before he found an inconspicuous announcement, placed in the New York Times by her parents when she married a year ago. Her husband was an art teacher at a secondary school in London. No fame, no family name. Nae had gained a little weight and cut her hair short, but she was still just as beautiful. The man had his arm around her, and she was smiling, radiantly happy.

It was then, on the grandest day of his own life, that he realized: even if he became the most successful man in his parents' eyes and his own, to Nae he had only ever been a convenience of the moment.

He was nothing more than the bottle of white wine he'd bought back then from the bottom shelf of a corner deli in New York, too sweet and too cheap, nothing more than that bottle of Oyster Bay. He would simply have to accept the one fact: the only person he had ever loved had never loved him.
