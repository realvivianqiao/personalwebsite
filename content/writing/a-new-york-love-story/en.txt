He hadn't really thought about her in years, and then he thought of her at his own wedding. The reason was ridiculous. One of his wife's friends had had too much to drink and was arguing with the bartender: "What do you mean you're out of white wine? You just twist the cap off! It's the easiest wine there is, how can you be out?" He was the groom, surrounded by friends and family, and the words drifted over his shoulder, through all the deafening congratulations, and reached him. At the worst possible moment, he understood. To her, he had been exactly that. Cheap, convenient, just respectable enough to put on the table. The white wine they sell in every corner deli in New York, the kind that opens with one twist.

They had met somewhere classic: Central Park, by the statue at Fifty-Ninth and Fifth.

The first time he saw Central Park was the winter he was fourteen, when his parents took him to New York. Why winter? Because the flights were cheaper, of course. On the way home they got caught in a blizzard and their flight was delayed by a whole day. His mother argued with United until they put the family up in a hotel near the park. The park hadn't even been on his parents' list, next to the World Trade Center and Wall Street and the Empire State Building, and they grumbled about it under their breath. It's just a big park, there are parks all over California. Why couldn't they give us a hotel near Wall Street?

Wall Street had been the first stop on that trip. They didn't even drop their bags before his father took him to touch the bronze bull. His father watched the men in sharp suits hurrying past and the long black cars, his face naked with envy, then squeezed his son's hand and said, "We crossed an ocean and went through hell to come to this country so you could have a better life. If you don't make something of yourself, you'll have let us down."

His father's idea of making something of yourself was well ahead of the people around him. He'd studied physics at Tsinghua and was clever to a fault. He brought his wife, who was beautiful but hadn't had much schooling, to America full of confidence, to do a PhD in theoretical physics at a middling school in California. To keep them fed, his mother worked at a Chinese restaurant in town. Now and then a man would whistle at her or ask for her number, and she'd roll her eyes. My husband is going to be a professor at an American university! As if the likes of you could compare. But after two years his father finally admitted that he wasn't cut out for research. He scraped through his degree and switched to computers. Google didn't exist yet and the World Wide Web was still a novelty. His father started as a programmer at a startup, sold his shares after a few years, and moved to a bigger company. Today those shares would be worth an astonishing amount, and his mother still sighs about it whenever it comes up, though at the time she probably didn't know what a share was. Still, the new company did well. He didn't get much equity, but he stayed, and despite his poor English and his awkwardness with people, sheer hard work got him to engineering manager, with a team of twenty-odd under him. It wasn't enough for his father. To really make it, you couldn't spend your life hunched over a computer writing code. You had to go into banking, into finance, and boss around those arrogant Americans.

He'd thought about it and decided his father had two reasons. First, his father's English really was bad, and he was small and slight, with round glasses thick as the bottoms of bottles. In his early years in America he'd been looked down on and pushed around plenty. When Americans gave him grief (usually a strange smile from a cashier or a mailman), he could never think of a comeback, so he came home and took it out on his son. Second, the senior student his father had most admired during his PhD was on Wall Street. That man spoke beautiful English and was tall and handsome. He'd dropped out of the PhD halfway through for Lehman Brothers, and when Lehman collapsed he moved to Barclays, shuttling between New York and London, very senior. "Married a blonde, blue-eyed American, has a whole team of Americans under him, living like a king." Those were his father's words.

He hadn't grown up rich, exactly, but as an only child he'd never wanted for anything. Yet the old-fashioned thinking his parents had brought from China never changed: you spend money where it counts and not a cent more. So that winter when he was fourteen, a carriage ride in the park was out of the question, a resort with a spa pool and breakfast was out of the question, and a Michelin restaurant was out of the question entirely. But anything to do with his making something of himself, they never spared a cent. The best prep school, philosophy and history summer programs in Europe, the top business school, golf, skiing, piano, violin, handmade Italian suits and shoes. Nothing was too much.

So when he graduated from Wharton with an offer from Goldman's TMT group in New York, he wasn't sure whether living somewhere nice counted toward making something of himself, and he called his parents to ask what kind of place he should rent. They agreed: as long as it's safe, the cheaper the better, and you should start investing early. So he and two friends found a prewar walk-up whose long, narrow windows faced north. In the daytime, with the lights off, the place looked like a nest built to some termite's master plan. The three of them shared one bathroom. There was no dishwasher, and the laundromat was across the street. But as his mother said, who's going to see it except you and your roommates?

What his mother didn't know was that when he brought girls home, they would see it. At first he was a little embarrassed, but then he realized they didn't care at all, and after that he didn't hold back. He'd been in New York two years before he met her, and in that time he'd brought home at least fifty girls. Girls from bars and clubs, from company events, from parties thrown by friends of friends, and now and then from the apps.

He was tall to begin with, and good-looking enough. He'd played football in high school and rowed crew for three years in college, so he was built, which was rare for an Asian guy. His looks alone were enough for plenty of women, and then there was the school and the job: a young man going places. So many women wanted something serious with him, even marriage, before they'd even met him. Who was going to care where he lived? Not that he had any intention of getting married, or even of having a girlfriend. Why would a man like him, one in a million, tie himself to one woman? He was careful never to say so to the women he dated. They all thought he was warm and sunny and devoted, with a bright future ahead of him, one in a million. Half of that was because he could talk a good game. The other half was probably because they wanted to believe it. What do women fall in love with, after all, if not an idea?

He was sure of himself with New York women, and then he met her and lost. They matched on that app. They chatted a little and he asked her out, for no special reason. Work was busy, his life was short on women, and she seemed young and pretty and a little naive. He figured one or two dates and he'd get her home. That was all.

He still remembers the first time he saw her. It was June. He'd changed trains twice and dodged three homeless men and arrived out of breath and sweating through his shirt, while she strolled over from a luxury building less than five minutes away without a drop of sweat on her, gave him a dazzling smile and said, "Hi! I'm Nae."

He still thinks Nae was beautiful, and not the way his wife is beautiful. For his wife to look beautiful to him, she has to put on a custom dress from her favorite designer, push her breasts into the right shape, spend an hour on her makeup, curl her hair into the perfect wave and arrange her face into a gracious, attentive smile. That girl wore a white sundress, a canvas tote and big sunglasses, and she took his breath away. Even in New York, a city full of beautiful women, he'd have given her at least an eight.

On that first date they took the horse carriage, because Nae thought it would be fun. She studied literature at Columbia and was half Korean, half American. He assumed her mother was Korean and her father American; it turned out to be the other way round. She'd grown up moving between Korea, the States and England, and said she was an old soul. She loved jazz and old movies, and lately she'd been obsessed with documentaries about plane crashes. When the carriage stopped at a busy corner, she pulled out her phone, delighted. She'd just downloaded a magnetic field detector and wanted to see if there were any strong magnetic fields around.

Since her father was Asian too, he told her about his strict parents and the jokes Asian kids make about being the model student, assuming she'd been through the same thing. But she was the youngest daughter, and her parents had never put much pressure on her. Since she'd gone to high school in London, he told her about his semester abroad at Oxford, when he'd gone down to Soho every weekend and hit every club there. She agreed, politely, that the music was good. Since she minored in art history, he told her about the Yayoi Kusama show he'd just seen (no need to say which girl he'd gone with; a hint was enough to show how desirable he was). Dimples appeared in her cheeks, and she asked who his favorite artist was. He said confidently that it had to be either David Hockney or Andy Warhol, and added a few lines he'd picked up from the audio guide. She only smiled and didn't say anything.

He thought he'd figured out New York women. One look and he knew what kind of woman she was and how to play her. The insecure ones got three texts a day. The literary ones got Goethe and Murakami. The practical ones got his degrees and his job and weekend golf with colleagues at the country club. The simple ones got told they were the most beautiful woman he'd ever seen. He had a counter for every move, it never failed, and he was almost always the one steering the conversation.

But that day, in that jolting carriage, and so many times afterward, his brain simply stopped working. He couldn't figure her out at all. Nothing she said gave him anything to go on, and he found himself genuinely curious about who she really was.

Her apartment was big and beautiful, with floor-to-ceiling windows over the whole of Central Park and large, lovely paintings on the walls. But in the short time they were together, while he held forth about contemporary art and went on and on about who he'd run into at the country club, she only smiled and agreed now and then. She couldn't be bothered to give her own opinion. She couldn't be bothered to mention that the paintings were her mother's, or that her father owned the building. She couldn't be bothered to ask about anything to do with him. Whether his parents had made him play violin or piano, whether he was a member at the country club, whether he preferred swimming or tennis, what he'd dreamed of as a boy, what had hurt him as a child, how many girlfriends he'd had, even who he really was, what he really thought, what he really wanted: none of it interested her. She'd ask, as a matter of routine, how his week had been and whether work was busy. All she needed was someone, a man she found decent enough and presentable enough, to go with her to the designer shops in SoHo, try the new restaurants in Brooklyn, see the latest show at a friend's gallery. Or just to sit with her on the deep Minotti sofa, both of them a little drunk, watching a documentary about why Air France Flight 447 went down, listening to Billie Holiday, watching nineties London stand-up. That was all.

It lasted less than six months, and in the end it was Nae who called it off. He came over as usual, and she opened a bottle of sauvignon blanc he'd picked up at the deli on his last visit. It was called Oyster Bay. Its cap was the grey-blue of an overcast sky and came off with a light twist, and the dark green bottle looked rather classy, though it cost less than thirteen dollars. Great value.

Sipping that cloying wine, she told him, as if it were nothing, that she'd decided to move to London after graduation and work for a while at a gallery run by a friend of her mother's, so they probably wouldn't see each other after that. He could transfer to the London office, he said. He actually said that. But she shook her head and only repeated, "We probably won't see each other after that." She changed her number, she never posted anything online, and he never saw Nae again.

He dated plenty of women afterward. But for a long time, every woman he met made him think of Nae. All of them, mascaraed lashes fluttering, glossy lips moving, slim waists swaying, were the same. They talked about the same things, thought the same things, wanted mostly the same things. Only Nae, cut off mid-scene in his memory, stood apart.

At the time he'd been absolutely certain why she didn't want to stay with him. He just wasn't successful enough, couldn't measure up to the people in her world. His mother, who understood how these things worked, sighed when she heard about it. "Our families were never a match. It would have been marrying up."

His wife was marrying up too, as his mother put it. She wasn't beautiful, to be honest, but her father had founded a private equity firm and her grandfather was a well-known name in New York politics. She met him at an alumni event and was very taken with him; when he heard her last name, he was a hundred times more taken with her. Then came the dating, the proposal, and now, after every obstacle, the wedding. He had always worked hard, and now he had wings. Still young, he'd made a name for himself; there wasn't an Asian person in finance who didn't know who he was.

He had everything he'd wanted when he met Nae. Status, money, making something of himself by his parents' simple definition: he had all of it. And yet, out of nowhere, he thought of Nae. He slipped away from the crowd, took out his phone and searched her name, and several pages in he found it, a small wedding announcement her parents had placed in the New York Times when she married a year ago. Her husband was an art teacher at a secondary school in London. No fame, no family name. Nae had put on a little weight and cut her hair short, but she was as beautiful as ever. The man had his arm around her, and she was smiling, radiantly happy.

It was only then, on the biggest day of his own life, that he understood. Even if he became the most successful man in his parents' eyes and his own, to Nae he had only ever been a convenience.

He was the bottle of white wine he'd bought from the bottom shelf of a corner deli in New York, too sweet and too cheap. He was that bottle of Oyster Bay. And he would have to accept the one fact that remained: the only person he had ever loved had never loved him.
