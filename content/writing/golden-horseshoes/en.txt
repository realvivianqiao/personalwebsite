^ "Some people are content with their fate and some are not, but that hardly matters. What matters is how you bear it."
^— Albert Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus

The horse kept seeing the same person at the same corner. The man with the whip always stopped at that corner to look for fares, and while they waited, the horse could look around. Across the street stood a tall billboard, and on it was a horse far finer and stronger than this one, its long mane sleek and flowing, its coat glossy, without a single scar from the whip. It stood on its hind legs with its forelegs raised, ready to spring, and its hooves gleamed with golden horseshoes.

For a horse, a person is much harder to remember than a corner. When this person had found a place in the horse's head, and why, even the horse couldn't have said.

All it remembered was first noticing this person one winter evening. It was cold, a passenger was haggling with the man with the whip, and the horse stamped impatiently. The sun was half down, and its last light fell across the filthy, slushy street and, generously, on the person standing under the billboard. The horse's eyesight was poor. It couldn't tell whether this person was beautiful or ugly by human standards, or make out the expression on their face. But in all its years of watching humans, it had learned to recognize someone who didn't stand out, and this person didn't stand out at all. If they ever tried to haggle with the man with the whip, they'd be flattened, and probably left speechless.

The horse never took part in the haggling, but it always judged in silence. There was a set of scales in its heart, and they always tipped toward whoever was weaker. Not out of any sense of justice. The horse simply wanted the argument over a few coins to end sooner, and the moment one side weakened, the white flag was on its way. The end of the argument meant pulling the carriage and its passengers down the same road again and being whipped again. The horse knew that. Waiting at the corner or out on the avenue, it would never get to wear those golden shoes. It was only a switch from one meaningless state to another, neither better nor worse, the same underneath, like cutting water with a sharp knife. Even so, the horse was willing to walk the same road over and over. Less resignation than release.

The horse had grown up in a stable by the river. The men with the whip came and went, and so did the horses beside it. At first it tried to remember every name, human and horse, until the last horse it had grown up with died, and then it saw how pointless that was and gave up. The horse had seen a great deal of the world. It had never read a book, of course, but it had heard a lot of stories and walked a lot of roads. The story that stayed with it most was the one about the dashing American and the green light. What's so special about a green light? the horse wondered, listening to a father tell it to his little girl in the back seat. There's a green light right up ahead. This avenue is full of them.

After that, the horse saw this person several more times, in different seasons. One sweltering summer day a storm broke, and the man with the whip lashed the horse to make it hurry. As it ran past the billboard it glimpsed the person again, standing under it with an umbrella. Back in the stable, shaking off the rain, the horse had an odd thought: Could they be waiting for me? Then the man with the whip brought the hay, and the horse forgot about it.

The last time the horse saw this person was late one night the following winter. The horse was old by then, its belly thinner, its step slower, but the man with the whip had no plans to retire it. It came back to the stable after another long day. Most of the others were asleep. It leaned against the wall, head down, quietly chewing its hay.

That was when the person appeared. First the horse heard footsteps coming closer, one heavy and one light. Then a pair of golden boots stopped in front of its trough. They were spotless and threw back the glare of the streetlamp. The horse had seen all kinds of boots, low Chelsea boots, padded mid-calf boots, boots that came over the knee, and each kind kicked its belly a little differently. It didn't yet know how brand-new golden boots would feel against its belly; it had never seen golden boots before. That made sense, it supposed. Whether you're on a horse or in a carriage, you don't want to get your shoes dirty, and gold is such a showy, impractical color.

The horse looked up and saw who it was. How did the horse know this was the same person? It couldn't have explained. All it knew was that no one but the person in front of it could lose an argument so badly, even to the greenest man with a whip. He was unremarkable to look at, with a black travel bag on his back and a grey padded coat. A middle-aged man, the horse decided, on his way to going bald. The golden boots looked absurd on him. Looking down, the horse saw that he was lame, too. One leg was noticeably thinner than the other, and one shoulder sat noticeably lower under the thick coat.

Suddenly the man spoke. "I can set you free." His voice was high and thin, like a boy's before it breaks. "I've watched you working this city for too long, and I can't bear it. I'm not able to do anything bigger, but I'd like you at least to be free of the whip before you die."

"A horse like you shouldn't be shut in a stable, hauling passengers, taking the lash. Even tying up your tail and racing you would be beneath you," the man went on. "You should be galloping across open grassland, or at least running free down this avenue."

The horse was an ordinary Belgian, and it knew it had no fine bloodline and no special gifts. Why would it matter so much to this man? The horse wasn't going to fall for it. It had seen the world. Most likely he was another one who wanted to save it, another monkey pulling a fish out of the water so it wouldn't drown. The horse had met plenty of those. They wanted to be saved by fate by saving it, to be praised by the world by praising it. In the end they were no different from the man with the whip; they didn't care what the horse wanted, only what they could get through it. No, they were worse. The man with the whip didn't pretend. These people made a show of caring what the horse wanted, pressed some gift on it, and walked away with the selfish glow of a dream come true. But was this man one of them?

The horse studied him from under its long lashes. His beard was patchy, his features soft and round, his face pale, his hair almost gone. It wasn't much of a manly face. But in his small grey eyes there seemed to be real concern.

"Golden horseshoes," the horse said. "Not grey ones."

The man blinked. "Golden horseshoes?"

The horse nodded. "Golden horseshoes. That's all I want."

The man said nothing. He stepped back, pulled up the hood of his coat, and turned and limped away. The horse sighed through its nostrils. It went back to its hay, chewing slowly, tasting how little it tasted of. The others were fast asleep. They were still in their prime, at the age when you can eat well and sleep well and work hard the next day without having to think about anything.

The horse knew its fate perfectly well. It was, as it had said, nothing but a switch between two states, waiting for passengers and carrying them. Even galloping free across the grassland would only add a third state, one pool of water flowing into another, meaningless all the same, and on its feet the same grey, rusted shoes. The horse didn't want freedom. It didn't even know what freedom was. All it wanted was golden horseshoes. It wanted to be the splendid horse on the billboard.

But the man came back. When he came running back, out of breath, the horse had no idea how long he'd been gone. The night was thicker and the air colder. Running had put color in his pale face, and the little hair he had was stuck to his forehead with sweat. Something glittered in his hands. Four golden horseshoes.

They were exactly like the ones on the billboard, more beautiful, even. Only real gold has that light, soft and delicate and yet fragile and blinding.

The man climbed into the stall, careful not to wake the others. Quickly and deftly he pried off the horse's old rusted shoes and fitted the new ones. He said he'd been lame since he was a boy, but luckily he could ride; he'd grown up on horseback and even ridden in competitions. The horse listened without a word, looking at the old shoes lying in the mud and dung where he'd dropped them. To think that filthy stuff had been part of its body for so many years.

The man stroked the horse's mane and said goodbye. He climbed out of the stall, looked back at it once, and was gone. That was the last time the horse saw him.

The next day, on the television news, the man saw the horse. "Horse rears in frightening incident; three passengers slightly hurt." The driver was still shaken, but with a camera on him for once he couldn't keep the excitement out of his voice. "This old horse is as gentle as they come. Pulled people for decades without a single problem. I don't know what got into it today. It just spooked at that corner out of nowhere, went straight up on its hind legs and started screaming, and there was no holding it."

The picture cut to a photo a passerby had snapped from across the street. The horse was old and thin and dirty, the whip scars showing through its brown coat, but its pose was exactly that of the billboard behind it: hind legs planted, forelegs raised, ready to spring, and on its hooves the gleaming golden shoes. The passengers in the carriage had gone white, the argument over a few coins a minute earlier long forgotten. Before the police could step in with force, the horse collapsed. From rising into the air to falling took no more than five seconds. The picture cut to a huge excavator trying to scoop up the body and haul it away.

The segment ended quickly. A horse dying was still a bit of a novelty; if it had been a person, it probably wouldn't have made the news at all. Then came an ad for juice, bright colors, empty lines, cheerful, monotonous music. Plump, juicy strawberries and blueberries, a pretty actor in a crisp pink shirt, and none of the wars or pain or struggle in the world existed. "Taste nature at its purest. Every drop is a hug from life!"

The man didn't know whether to be happy or sad. Whether he had been a savior or a bystander, he had no way of knowing.
