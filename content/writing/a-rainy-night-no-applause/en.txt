He flicked away his cigarette. The last sparks met a raindrop and went out with a hiss in a tiny puff of steam, and the ash dissolved into the drizzle. Bicycles going past had splashed the hems of his trousers, and now and then someone under an enormous umbrella turned to look at him. He had just woken from a long, warm, enormous dream, and he no longer knew how long he'd been standing here, straight-backed under this slanting eave. The sky was low and bottomless, and beyond it lay black water just as endless. Out there someone might be drowning, he thought, looking at the sea and its occasional ripples. She's thrashing, screaming herself hoarse, and nobody hears. Nobody cares about one person going under. The world is too big.

In the dream, so many things had been simplified in ways that made no sense. That world was small, drawn in single lines, built like a staircase, and yet it was sweet. It ran on Pavlov's simple feedback: give the expected response and you got your reward. Every move had an audience. He must have done some strange, contrary things in that dream too, but they were only stunts; the point was attention. Later he figured out how it worked. If you wanted applause in the spotlight, you'd better follow the rules and keep going down the road that had been laid for you. So he walked it, step by step, but there were fewer and fewer people beside him and fewer and fewer watching. The applause was still there, only thinner, and farther away.

When did I wake up?

The rain stopped, and so did his last cigarette. He couldn't remember when he'd started smoking. Did I wake up because nobody was clapping, or because I got tired of the dream?

The sky turned greyer and the line between water and sky blurred, and the rising moon lit up the black water. The sea in the dream looked a lot like this one, he thought. At least the sea could always comfort him.

But he couldn't stand here staring at the sea forever. He left that small shelter in that huge city and walked deeper in. The streetlights here were a cold white at night; in the dream, the evening streets were always washed in a dim, warm yellow. He must have been good-looking, and the daze of having just woken gave him something of a wandering poet's air, so passersby kept glancing at him. He didn't notice, or didn't care. They were strangers on his night walk; no one was going to happen to walk this road with him all the way. When it came down to it, he was as indistinct as anyone in this city could be. His looks and his past, however particular, were shared with a thousand others. In the great machine of capitalism he wasn't that special, and he wasn't that hard to replace. What he'd achieved in that sweet dream after crossing mountains and rivers was where a thousand people here started out, where even their parents' dreams began. Nobody here would applaud because he existed, and hardly anyone would be heartbroken if he left.

It was fully dark now, and night had settled over the city. He stopped at the theater on the corner. Dancers were coming out the stage door, heavy coats over their shoulders. The last girl was beautiful, a proud little head on a slender neck. He looked past the crowd and watched her; everything she did was captivating. Admirers and fans lined up to hand her flowers, and her lovely face broke into a smile, but it couldn't quite hide how tired she was, how fed up.

In her own life, is she always the performer? If the audience disappeared, would she still dance? He badly wanted to ask her, but he didn't know how to explain a question that came out of nowhere. She'd probably fob me off with something rehearsed, he thought, about her love of dance being pure. But if she were the one drowning out there, would nobody hear her either?

As soon as he thought it, he laughed at himself. What a strange, nasty idea. She was obviously the most radiant, happiest person on the block. He looked once more at her beautiful face and walked away.

He wandered, glancing now and then into windows where someone had forgotten to draw the curtains. Most of the rooms had huge floor-to-ceiling windows and huge televisions to match. Couples lived here, probably, or newlyweds, maybe with a cat or a dog. Late at night after rain is the time to curl up at home with someone you love and watch something: a trashy soap, a bizarre documentary, an old movie badly in need of restoring. The couples he'd met, in the dream and in this world, were all strangely alike. They were all happy to do boring things together. Can love make boring things mean something new? he wondered. Even the most interesting thing feels wrong with someone you don't like. So doesn't love just give us permission to waste time? In a warm little nest, after all, you don't need to go rushing off after truth.

He saw a fat shiba inu in the next window and stopped. In the dream he'd just woken from there had been a chubby little dog like that. She always ran to him, delighted, tail wagging. She had her moods too, when she wouldn't come however he called, only curled up into a ball with her nose buried in her tail, twitching her ears now and then. But even when she was sulking, even when she was so lost in play that she forgot everything else, she always looked back to check he was still there, and if he fell too far behind she stopped and waited. She loved me for myself, he thought. She never expected me to become anything, never cared what I achieved. She wouldn't have been disappointed if I was lazy, wouldn't have run off if I was poor. I didn't have to be great or special at all to have her love and her loyalty, no conditions. But back then I was the one who got tired of a love that true. So why do I remember how warm it was now?

He bowed his head. The memories came at him all at once, and he had to crouch down.

Little ants were running in the cracks between the red bricks. He remembered that in the dream, when he was small, his favorite way to spend a hot afternoon, one after another, was watching ants carry off cookie crumbs. If I were still in the dream, would I be happier? he asked himself. And is happiness what I'm meant to be after? If someone stayed in the dream forever, would he be lucky, or as unlucky as a person can be?

He stood up, a little dizzy, and rubbed his temples. There was no one anywhere. He wanted to shout, to sob, to run as hard as he could, but in the end he did none of it. He only shut his eyes tight and stood still while a boundless loneliness swallowed him, bit by bit, without a sound.
