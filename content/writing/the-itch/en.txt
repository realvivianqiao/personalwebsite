The itch came out of nowhere.

She was up early as always, out the door at seven-thirty sharp, and the whole subway ride she ran through the morning's client meeting and her pitch in her head. This was her most important prospect. She'd called in favors for the introduction, fought her bosses to be given the account, and stayed late night after night preparing. Her year, and every promotion after it, rode on this. She could not mess it up.

The meeting was at nine-thirty. At nine-twenty she was in the restroom checking her makeup one last time. Three rounds of mouthwash; a flawless smile. She ran through the pitch she knew cold, took a breath, and pulled open the door.

That was when the itch started.

It was on the outside of her right forearm, about an inch below the elbow. She gave it a quick scratch through the sleeve of her sharp navy suit. It didn't do much, but she didn't think about it.

She went into the conference room and shook hands down the line. By the third hand the itch was still there. Her stomach dipped, but she couldn't scratch now, so she kept her face perfectly still and moved on to the fourth hand, the fifth, the last. They all had big, hearty voices and made the kind of jokes they thought were harmless. She laughed along.

When the pleasantries were done, the itch hadn't eased at all. She walked to the front of the room, and the moment her back was turned she scratched at it through her jacket with her left hand. Nothing. Now she was starting to panic. An itch normally vanished after one light scratch, but this one had dug in and wouldn't let go. Fine, she told herself. Hold out. It's one hour. After that you can scratch all you want. She put on her professional smile and, in that lovely voice of hers, walked them through market conditions and historical returns.

But the itch only got worse. It was like a clown desperate for attention, capering around her and tickling her already frayed nerves with a feather fan. With all those eyes on her, how could she scratch? She held it in, left hand pressed to the seam of her trousers, every muscle clenched, a fine sweat breaking out along her hairline. She raised her right hand toward the big five-year annualized return on the screen. She was talking faster now, and her voice had begun to shake.

The itch. It went right through her. Something a fingernail could normally settle in a second had become torture. She wanted nothing more than to rip off the jacket and dig in with her long sharp nails, scratch until there was skin packed under them. Risk and return, asset classes, investment strategy, promotions, raises: one by one they drained out of her head, until she no longer knew where she was or what she was doing, or why she had ever wanted a promotion so badly. There was only the itch. She couldn't think of anything but scratching it.

Her right hand fell away from the returns. Her voice stopped mid-sentence. Before anyone at the table understood what was happening, she had taken off her jacket in front of all of them, dropped it on the floor, tucked her right arm against her stomach, bowed her head, and started scratching with her left hand, completely absorbed. At first she scratched in a steady rhythm, back and forth. When that didn't work, she braced her thumb against her arm and raked with the other four fingers, hard and patient, like someone working a field. The room was silent except for the scrape of her nails on her skin. By now her arm was a mess: a wide red patch, the skin broken, a few long, angry welts beaded with blood. But the itch was mostly gone, and she let out her breath.

She looked up. No one in the room said a word. They were all staring, mouths open, as if they'd just witnessed something that happens once in a hundred years. Only then did she come back to herself. She snatched up her jacket and tried to explain, but she couldn't get a single word out. There was no script for this. Nothing she said would help.

Outside the glass door stood her boss and her coworkers. Her boss was livid; her rivals were trying not to grin. Past them, a TV on the far wall was playing the news: "Gold prices surge, and funds with 'gold' in their names surge with them." And then, just like that, the itch was gone, completely. It had come without warning, and it left the same way.

How absurd, she thought, and fled.
