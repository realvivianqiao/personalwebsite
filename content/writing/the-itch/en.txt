The itch came out of nowhere.

She woke early as usual, stepped out the door at seven-thirty on the dot, and took the subway to work, rehearsing the run of today's client meeting and her pitch the whole way. This was the most important prospect she had. She had called in favors to get the introduction and fought her bosses hard to be given the lead, then stayed late night after night preparing. Her numbers this year, and every promotion after, hung on it. She absolutely could not blow it.

The meeting was at nine-thirty. At nine-twenty she was in the restroom, checking one last time that her makeup was perfect. She had rinsed with mouthwash three times; her smile was flawless. She ran through the pitch she knew by heart once more, took a deep breath, and pulled open the door.

That was when the itch arrived.

It started on the outside of her right forearm, three centimeters below the elbow. She gave it a quick scratch through the sleeve of her sharply cut navy suit. It didn't do much, but she didn't think anything of it.

She walked into the conference room and went around shaking hands. By the third hand, the itch still hadn't gone. Something inside her flinched, but she didn't dare scratch now, so she kept her face perfectly composed and went on to the fourth hand, the fifth, the last. The owners of the hands all had big, booming voices and made the kind of jokes they considered harmless. She laughed along.

The pleasantries ended, and the itch was still there, showing no sign of letting up. She went to the front of the room, and the moment her back was turned she scratched it a few times through her jacket with her left hand. Still nothing. She was starting to panic. Normally an itch vanished without a trace after one light scratch. This one was different; it simply refused to leave. Nothing to be done, she told herself. Hold on. The meeting was only an hour. After that she could scratch as much as she liked. She put on her standard professional smile and, in her lovely voice, with all the right rises and falls, walked them through market conditions and historical returns.

But the itch only got worse, like a clown who will do anything for attention, leaping around and tickling with a feather fan at nerves already rubbed raw by the pressure. With so many pairs of eyes on her, how could she possibly scratch the way she wanted to? All she could do was bear it. She pressed her left hand flat against the seam of her trousers and tensed every muscle in her body. Tiny beads of sweat broke out on her forehead. She raised her right hand and pointed at the big five-year annualized return on the screen, and she found herself talking faster, her voice starting to shake.

The itch. An itch that went straight to the bone. The kind of thing a fingernail normally settles with one light stroke had become, at this moment, torture. What she wanted more than anything was to tear off the suit jacket and really go at it with her long, sharp nails, scratch until flakes of skin were packed under them. Risk and return, asset classes, investment strategy, promotions and raises: slowly all of it faded from her mind. She no longer knew where she was, or what she was doing, or why she had ever worked so hard for a promotion and a raise. In their place was the only thing she could still think about, the itch. Apart from wanting to scratch, she could think of nothing at all.

The right hand pointing at the returns dropped. Her voice stopped mid-sentence. Before anyone around the table had registered it, she had taken off her suit jacket in front of them all, dropped it on the floor, tucked her right arm across her stomach, bowed her head, and begun to scratch with her left hand, with total concentration. First she scratched back and forth, steady, in rhythm. When that didn't do much, she braced her thumb against her arm and set the other four fingers to work together, hard and patient, like tilling a field. The room was very quiet. The only sound was the scrape, scrape of her sharp nails on her skin. Her arm, of course, had long since become a horror: a wide patch of red, the skin broken, several long, ugly welts beading with blood. But under that assault the itch had mostly gone, and she let out a breath.

She lifted her head. The conference room was silent, everyone staring, mouths open, as if they had just witnessed a once-in-a-century spectacle. Only then did she come back to herself. She snatched up her jacket and opened her mouth to explain, stammering, but not a single word came out. No workplace script covered this. Nothing she could say would help.

Outside the glass door stood her boss and her colleagues. Her boss was livid; her rivals were beaming. She looked past them to a television mounted in the distance, which was running the news: "Gold prices soar, and funds with 'gold' in their names soar with them." And then, all at once, the itch was gone, completely. It had come without warning, and it left the same way.

"How absurd!" She fled.
