^ "Most people, Kamala, are like falling leaves that drift and turn in the air and come swaying down to the ground. But others, a few, are like stars that travel a fixed course; no wind can reach them."
^— Siddhartha

Another winter night in New York, the end of January, the wind howling and beating on the glass, so bitter it leaves no room for hope. I toss and turn. Whenever the sky goes grey I think of his law, and this winter it has proved itself again and again. The clock hands have just passed twelve. Lights dot the dark outside the window, and the night covers everything. January 31, I say to myself. Exactly five years since he left this world. These five years I have been busy and empty, my life going in circles, with nothing left of the high spirits I had when I first met him. "Méditation" is playing in the corner of the room, the D major as serene as a sea in sunlight. I close my eyes, and I am back in that winter five years ago.

## 1

That winter I had just graduated early, with honors, from Columbia's computer science department. My student years had been dutiful and smooth, and in April I was going to start at the biggest tech company there is. I figured I would use the break before my start date to see a little of the world. There was nowhere I absolutely had to go; I just wanted somewhere warm to look at the sea, so I settled on Puerto Rico, on the Caribbean. Close, and cheap.

The inn I had booked was on a street right by the beach, a street of nearly identical surf hostels, none taller than four stories, their walls a cheerful yellow or green and covered in vines. The name of mine stood out, though. Not some long, tongue-twisting Spanish name, just "Kenny." My flight landed in the middle of the night, and it was almost two in the morning by the time I got there. Luckily the weather was lovely: no wind, warm without being sticky. I was quietly glad to have escaped that cold city of steel and concrete for a while, so even though the front door was locked, and I knocked for a long time before anyone answered, I wasn't annoyed.

A man in his fifties came to let me in, yawning. At first I took him for a guest here to surf: deeply tanned, muscular arms, shaggy light hair, a baggy cotton tank top, board shorts down to his knees, flip-flops slapping. But he said he was the owner, his name was Ian, and he took me to the front desk to check in.

Ian took my ID, fished a pair of glasses out of his pocket, perched them on his nose and held the card at arm's length. "From New York?" I nodded. As he typed my details into an ancient computer, he mentioned that he had lived in New York once too. I was surprised and asked what he had done there. He waved it off. Nothing interesting. When he was done he picked up a key and walked me to my room, warning me on the way, "It's a good season for surfing, but you still get storms. Last year somebody got pulled out by the sea and never came back. Better to be careful." Then he shuffled back downstairs in his flip-flops.

The room faced the sea. The window was open, and the air was damp and tasted of salt. As I unpacked I kept thinking about Ian. Ever since I was little I have loved reading books, and reading people. If I had infinite time I would read every book in the world and then study every person in it. But life is finite, so I can only read the books that interest me most and get to know the people who interest me most. And at first sight, Ian had my curiosity hooked. Never mind why he had left New York to run an inn here; the man himself was impossible to read. There was something about him I couldn't name. Or, thinking about it later, it was his eyes. When I met him I was yawning nonstop, my head foggy, the signal from retina to brain slowed to a crawl. Even so, I remember with unusual clarity the thought that shot through my mind when I first saw his eyes under the dim light of that narrow doorway: those are some eyes. Like the sky on a summer evening between two downpours, the sky in between, an ominous calm, as if something had been lost for good, or something was gathering.

For no reason I slept lightly that night, and woke early. After washing up I went down for breakfast. In last night's dim light I hadn't noticed, but out the back door of the inn was a small pavilion built on the sand, its floor raised about two meters above the beach, with two or three sets of rough wooden tables and chairs. Outside the sun was bright, the deep blue sea sparkled, and a few guests lay on the sand looking perfectly content. I picked up breakfast in the lobby and took it out to the pavilion. The only person there was Ian, eating alone at a corner table with his back to the sea, dressed the same as the night before.

He looked up, saw it was me, and said hello. "Morning! How'd you sleep?"

"Not bad! Sorry for checking in so late last night." I sat down across from him as I said it.

He waved a hand. "You're the guest. Check in whenever you like. You here on your own?"

I nodded and told him I had just graduated, so this was a graduation trip of sorts, and took a big bite of the local corn cake, toasted and crisp and delicious.

"Must be freezing in New York."

"Ridiculously cold! We got a few more big snowstorms last week. Good thing I came here!" I polished off both corn cakes in no time and washed them down with coffee. The coffee was very bitter; I had forgotten the sugar. "Speaking of New York, how long were you there?"

"Twenty-some years. Moved there after college, left a few years back."

"Twenty-some years! That's a long time! Did you run an inn in New York too?" The moment it was out of my mouth I regretted it. What a stupid question.

But this time he answered with surprising ease. "An inn in New York? Not easy! I was a trader. Bought and sold stocks and futures."

"A Wall Street big shot! So why'd you decide to come here?"

"Fate! Fate brought me here." He smiled and took cigarettes and matches out of his shorts pocket. "Smoke? Local tobacco. It's called Rivalo. Means rival in Spanish."

I took one and inhaled. The air filled with a smell like incense after it has burned out, and from the lobby behind us drifted "Méditation." I frowned.

"Don't like the cigarette?" he asked.

I shook my head. I don't smoke much, so I don't have opinions about cigarettes. This one was strong, but at least it didn't scratch my throat. "The cigarette's fine," I said. "It's the music I don't like."

"Massenet's 'Méditation.' The most famous piece from his opera Thaïs. A courtesan falls in love with a monk. For his sake she runs away from everything, leaves her old life, converts, and that's how the two of them end up together."

"It's a good piece, and the story behind it is very romantic," I said, nodding. "But when I was a kid my family made me take violin, and that was the piece I practiced most. My teacher was brutal. One wrong note and she'd rap my fingers with a little stick. Now whenever I hear it, all those painful memories come back."

"If it was that painful, didn't you ever think of running off?" he asked.

I took another sip of that horribly bitter coffee, regretted it at once, and smacked my lips. "I did run, actually. One lesson I just couldn't take it anymore. Halfway through I packed up my violin, picked up the case, and walked out of her house without looking back. Never went again. My parents gave me a real scolding, but I was thrilled."

He laughed and stubbed out his cigarette on his white china plate, already wiped clean, and the scorched smell of leftover bacon fat rose from it. "Bravo! Bravo! If you ask me, you did exactly right. Because when it comes down to it, there are only two kinds of people in this world: the ones who want to run, and the ones who don't. You see, you're one who wants to run. Round and round you go. You ran out of the practice room, and now you've run all the way here."

"That's so reductive! There are so many kinds of people in the world. Most of the ways we sort people, nationality, race, what you studied, what you do for work, have nothing to do with running or not running!"

"So it seems. But think about it carefully, and it's those categories that are reductive. They're only social attributes, made to serve the civilization of a particular era, and they carry too much of that era with them. Rigid is one thing, but they're also tedious and illogical. It's like shutting yourself inside the smallest shell of a Russian doll. Whenever someone asks who you are, you have to beg them to wait while you explain, layer by layer. Well, the outermost layer is nationality, the second is age, sex and skin color, the third is my job... Hang on, just five hundred more layers and you'll know the real me!

"And in the end you find the small shells turn into big ones and the big ones shrink into small ones, and a whole lot of other sizes turn up in your hands out of nowhere. So you have to take the dolls apart again, fit the big one over the small one and the small one over the smaller one, and hop obediently back into the smallest shell. Over and over, Sisyphus pushing his rock. So, leave the social attributes aside, and how do you know who someone is? Better to use the simplest measure, the one that goes straight to the heart: do you want to run, or not? Simple and clear, true in every age, carrying no era with it, no physiological or political or even scientific baggage. It holds everywhere."

Listening closely, I had to admit he had a point. Strip away the world's standards, and whatever personality type your music app or some quiz assigns you, and it would be best, it seems, to have something that defines a person across time and space. But to make "do you want to run or not" the golden rule was a bit too sweeping!

I objected. "Maybe the usual categories really are unreliable. But yours won't necessarily work either! Some people want to run because they have to, and once they get where they want to go they don't need to run anymore. Some people don't want to run only because there's nowhere to run to, or no need. Lumping them all together, isn't that a little arrogant?"

He shook his head. "Whether you can run is an objective constraint. Whether you are running is the outward form. But whether you want to run is what's closest to the human heart, to what a person is. There's no necessary cause and effect between the three. Take your violin. You probably weren't the only one who went through the same thing and hated it just as much, right? But didn't some of them want to skip, while others never even considered it?"

I thought about it, and that was true. My closest friend in elementary school had the same teacher. He wasn't especially gifted and he didn't especially love the violin, and the teacher scolded him plenty. But every weekend, without fail, he went for his two hours. When I ran into him a few days before the trip, he even played me Monti's "Csárdás." Still, there was a hole in his argument, so I asked, "But nothing's that absolute. Doesn't everyone, at some moment, at some point, more or less want to get away and go chasing something?"

He rested his elbow on the table and took a drag. "That's true. One choice doesn't prove anything. But the more of life's choices you make, the more you find that people who don't want to run are like those roly-poly dolls. Now and then they wobble, but their center of gravity is steady. Art, love, money, power, responsibility, any of them can be the center. Like Strickland. And the people who want to run can never find peace in the life they have. Running is what drives them forward. As they run, they're trying to find something that can truly give their souls peace. Trying to grab hold of something.

"Put it this way. Whether you can run looks like an objective constraint, but someone who wants to run doesn't consider whether they can. They don't even consider where they're running to. Running is the goal itself, the mission, the whole pursuit, the very meaning that holds their life up. Someone who wants to run has to run, even if it ends badly."

"But why do they have to run?" I was completely lost.

"Why do they have to run?"

He repeated it with a bitter little smile. "Wanting to run isn't up to you. It's written in your genes. Adenine and guanine tell you to run, and you have no choice but to run."

I was about to say more, but some guests had just arrived and he had to go see to them. As he left he said, "When there's time, I'll tell you my story."

## 2

I stayed another week at the surf inn. With nothing to do, I read in the pavilion or on the beach, and the whole time I kept turning him and his words over in my mind, more and more curious about his past. The only other person working there was a woman, a pretty local girl young enough to be his daughter, and she knew nothing about his background either. All she could say was that five years ago he had come here alone, opened the place, and never left. Ian was fifty at the very least. Surely he had some kind of family! But after a few days of watching I hadn't seen anyone who looked like a wife or a child come looking for him. In fact he lived by himself in a small room on the floor below mine. How strange!

I ran into him a few more times around the inn, but he was always busy and we only had time for a quick hello. Until one day of pouring rain. The guests were stuck inside, gathered in twos and threes in the lobby, reading, on their phones, chatting. I came downstairs, bought a beer and a little box of cashews at the bar, and planned to settle into the sofa in the corner facing the window and finish my book. I smelled the tobacco before I saw him.

He was sunk deep in that sofa, watching the rain and the sea through the window. On the coffee table in front of him sat half a bottle of bourbon, an ice bucket and an empty tumbler. Outside, the waves churned and the clouds piled up, the sky so dark it looked ready to cave in. The rain came down in sheets and poured into the black sea, and even through the glass you could feel the wind howling off the water. It had been mild and sunny a moment ago. The storm really had come out of nowhere.

"Finally caught you! Mind if I sit here?"

He turned his head, saw me, and shook it. I sat down on the sofa across from him.

"Storm's here. Don't go surfing," he said, half joking, and then, "What are you reading?"

"South of the Border, West of the Sun." I held up the cover to show him.

"What's it about?"

"A successful middle-aged man who almost leaves his wife and kids for his childhood sweetheart."

"And in the end he doesn't go?"

"He doesn't go."

"Why not?"

"His lover disappears, so he has nowhere to go, nowhere to run!" Remembering what he had said last time, I added, "By your logic, he's just someone who doesn't want to run. He'd already found the thing that gave him peace. He just lost it again."

"Lost it again." He repeated the words thoughtfully and leaned toward me a little, and only then did I notice how differently he was dressed from before: a long-sleeved blue button-down, khaki trousers, black loafers, his hair neatly combed, gold-rimmed glasses. He looked rather like a scholar.

"Why so dressed up?" I asked.

"I'm selling the place. Just came to terms with the buyer."

"With all these guests? Why sell?"

"The government wants to buy it and build a bigger resort. They made me a very good offer!"

"Ah. So what are you going to do after?" I set my book down on the side table.

He was quiet for a while, looked out the window, and asked, "Do you have time?"

I nodded.

He poured himself a drink. I held out the box of cashews; he took a few, put them in his mouth, sipped, and said, "Last time I said I'd tell you my story. Why not today. Do you know where I grew up?"

I shook my head.

He went on. "A very small place, between the mountains and the sea, and beautiful. My great-grandfather's generation fled there after the war and put down roots, and generation after generation had lots of children. We became a big family there, so big the city was named after us. The city had one main street, and running from west to east along it were the elementary school, the middle school, the high school, the university, city hall and the hospital. Most people back home spent their whole lives along that axis. The ones who liked school went all the way down it and became officials, doctors, professors. The ones who didn't turned off at some corner and opened a print shop, an inn, a restaurant or a bar, or just idled. But either way, they lived easy and content. Everyone was kind; nobody fought or grabbed; all the neighbors knew each other.

"My parents were childhood sweethearts, both very smart, both taught at the university. But my grades were average, I had no talent to speak of for sports or music, I wasn't good-looking, and there was nothing especially likeable about my personality. In short, I was as ordinary a kid as they come. Except that I loved to read. All kinds of books: novels, biographies, popular science, essays, philosophy... I read everything. But for all that reading I didn't get any smarter, and I couldn't remember a single profound line. I only felt that an afternoon in the library was like stepping into another world. When I wasn't reading, my favorite thing was diving and swimming in the sea. There was beautiful coral on the seabed there, far more beautiful than here. My days were so simple there was no need to record them: school, home, reading, swimming, sleeping, eating. My head was simple too. Nothing to think about.

"One perfectly ordinary afternoon the summer I was fourteen, I went down to the beach by our house after lunch for a swim. If anything was out of the ordinary, it was that there wasn't a single person on the sand. Maybe the sun was too fierce, and the grown-ups and kids had all gone in for a nap. The sun was burning my hair, there wasn't a sound anywhere, and the windless sea melted endlessly into the cloudless sky. I looked at that vast, familiar sea, and suddenly it felt strange to me, and I was confused. Since when had the sea been there, and why? And since when had I been here, and why? If the sea had never existed, would I not exist either? But if I didn't exist, would the sea still be there?

"I'd meant to go in and look at the coral. I already had my mask on. But all at once I felt a disgust for the coral that came from nowhere. It was only a heap of worm skeletons. I ran home, and there was no one there either. I sat in that empty, deathly silent house without moving, the whole afternoon.

"After that I started to feel disgust for the chattering girls and the loud boys in my class. Their studying, their fights, their smoking, their dating, their pop music, all of it bored me. I felt disgust for my parents. When they talked about the latest findings and which journals had taken their new papers, I couldn't have cared less. And in the end I felt disgust for my whole life. So this was it: such a small place, and every day such a repetitive, meaningless life.

"When that thought came, it was as if all the strength had been drained out of my body. I grabbed desperately for something to give me strength again, and then I found it. I had to leave this place. I had to do everything I could to get away from it, as far away as possible. I had to run. The longing to run became the engine of my life, pushing me forward with everything it had." He paused. "Do you understand what I mean?"

I nodded, took a sip of beer, and said, "You got sick of a monotonous life, so you wanted to see a bigger world?"

He nodded, then shook his head, and went on. "At first that's what I thought too. I thought I just wanted to see a different world, and ideally fall in love. I got into a university far from home, a very good one, and my parents were very proud. At that university I had my first girlfriend. She was in one of my classes, bold and beautiful. I fell for her at first sight, and as it happened she loved me deeply too. With her I smoked my first cigarette, met a crowd of friends I've had no contact with since, went to more parties than I could count, drank more in one year than in the twenty before it, raced down empty highways at four in the morning, and slept through class together in the back row the next day. We traded secrets, made promises, planned a future. She didn't like to read, but we never ran out of things to say.

"But after a while I started to find that life boring, and I started wanting to run again. It was right before graduation. I found a job in New York. I didn't think much about what the job actually was; I had no career ambitions to begin with. I just wanted to go as far as I could. I left her a letter, and then I was gone. New York was a long way from my university and from home. So far that what she looked like, the sound of her voice, the way she gathered up her hair, the curve of her mouth when she smiled, where she went afterward, I forgot all of it, completely."

"You just stopped loving her?" I said. "That's pretty cold."

"It isn't that I stopped loving her. I just couldn't hold back the urge to run. It flattened my love for her by an overwhelming margin." He sighed, took a clean grey lens cloth from his pocket, removed his glasses and bent his head to wipe them carefully, then set them on the little table beside him. And he went on. "The colder part comes later.

"I had no real talent, but I was inexplicably lucky. There seemed to be nothing I wanted to do that I couldn't pull off, and whatever I didn't want to do I could easily hand off to someone else. First I sold advertising, and my numbers were always good. Later a client introduced me to trading, and I was a trader for a long time. Trading could be terrifying, but I found it fascinating. I came up with a lot of strategies. The returns were excellent.

"In that time I got married and had two children, and I was a fairly decent husband and father. Money, time, feeling, I poured all of it into the family without holding back. I never cheated, never had the slightest interest in getting mixed up with other women. I never hit or yelled at my kids, and I never missed a single musical rehearsal or soccer game.

"My wife was a New Yorker. Most of her family were professors, like her. She was excellent at her job, and she also ran the household with total devotion and kept up our social ties for me. The head of the impossible-to-get-into private school was a friend of a friend; the director of the hospital had been her father's student. Our kids had no extraordinary talents, but at least they didn't make trouble at school or pick up any bad habits. Life was as perfect as the glossy rendering for a new housing development. Just thinking that I had a family to provide for filled me with strength. I often thought, this is how life should be, and I felt happy and satisfied with it. The engine that made me want to run had rusted from years of disuse."

He took another sip, rubbed the marks his glasses had left on the bridge of his nose, and continued. "One weekend the summer my older son was seven, my wife and kids happened to be away, and my friends were all busy with one thing or another. I had nothing to do, so I drove up to our house in the mountains for some fresh air. Just an unremarkable afternoon. I was sitting on the deck, staring into space, drinking a white wine from a friend's vineyard that he'd been saving. The air was damp; it had just poured. The clouds in the distance were heavy, and the dark green mountains went on and on. It was very quiet, just the occasional bird. Suddenly I remembered that hot summer noon so many years ago, standing alone and staring at the sea, and I remembered the years when I wanted to run, and all at once they seemed like a happy, unreal time. Do you understand? Every bit of effort, feeling, mind and time poured into one single thought, running, feeding it, making it grow. No matter what anyone else thought, no matter what reality was like, that was the theme of that stretch of your life and the whole of what you were after. I'm not sure even loving a person can be that pure."

That was true. I nodded.

"And then suddenly I felt that disgust again, the one that comes from nowhere. At first it was only a passing thought, like a single drop of oil leaking from a bottle in the storeroom whose cap wasn't screwed on tight. But then I discovered, in despair, that the strength was being drained out of me again. The oil had seeped into me, and the old, long-unused engine roared back to life. After that I couldn't help being disgusted by the things I used to care about. Whether the kids should learn riding or golf. Whether to go to Malta or St. Lucia for the winter. Whether my team had made the finals, why my favorite player had choked. Which of my wife's dresses looked better, who to invite to the family party. Soybeans or corn, oil or gold, sell or buy... I wanted to run again, to leave wherever I was. The desire was uncontrollable; it overwhelmed everything. But leaving then wasn't as easy and carefree as leaving after high school or college. I hesitated for a long time. First I quit my job. Then for about five years nothing seemed to change on the surface, but inside I was in agony, struggling, miserable. When the trust I'd set up for my family was more or less done, I left again."

I couldn't help cutting in. "Why did you have to leave? If you left after high school because you were tired of a monotonous life, and after college because you didn't love your girlfriend anymore, then this time you had everything. Why give all that up and leave?"

He looked out the window. The rain had eased, but the black clouds still pressed down on the sea. He looked back at me and said quietly, "Because I was afraid."

"Afraid?"

He nodded, and after a long silence said, "In those five years, whenever night fell and everything went quiet, I would think of the endless sea, the long ranges of mountains, the moon in the black sky. The sea, the mountains and the moon have always been there, from the Stone Age to the modern world. The ancient ice melts, an owl lands on a branch, fireworks tear across the night. They've always been there. But I'm gone in a flash. I can only live in this one era. At most I can break the limits of space; I can never break the limits of time. My life can only bear the brand of this era, can only have the few possibilities this era allows. And I'm supposed to accept, willingly, that out of those pitifully few possibilities I've already made my choices: trader, father, husband. Whenever I thought of that, of how pitifully few my possibilities were, and how little hope the future held, I was terrified."

"And running made you not afraid?"

"Running, for me, even if it was only through space, was still a glorified kind of hope, something to pin my possibilities on. Even if I didn't know where the possibility would take me, at least I was full of hope again, and felt I'd grabbed hold of something in the flood of fate."

I repeated what he said. "Grabbed hold of something."

He nodded. "I only told my wife once the trust paperwork was done. She cried in disbelief, cried until she shook, curled up on the sofa. She didn't understand why I was afraid, or why I had to go. She thought I was having an affair, thought I'd lost my mind, thought I was joking. She said she could go with me, and then she said if I left I should never come back. I was crying too. Half of it was guilt and helplessness. I had wronged them, but fate had pushed me to this point and I had to go. If I didn't, if I stayed and forced myself to keep playing the part of a decent father and husband, then I, as myself, would soon fall apart. The other half was that I envied my wife from the bottom of my heart. At least she had grabbed hold of something. Whatever it was, love for me and the kids, responsibility, how things look to the world, at least she'd found something she could hold on to tight in the flood of fate, and at least she was willing to keep hold of it and not let go. And I had nothing. I couldn't hold on to anything."

"So you keep running, again and again, to find something you can hold on to?" I asked.

He poured himself a little more and said, "Love wears thin, passion fades, even responsibility and conscience can break in a single moment. What I want to hold on to is something pure, with nothing mixed in, something constant. Whoever I am, whether I'm living in the past, the present or the future, the thing I want to hold on to should be the same one thing. That's what I want to find." He paused, then went on. "Back then I thought, for other people this thing might be music, or love, or money, or power, or even a professorship. But I hadn't found mine yet, so I had to keep looking."

The ice in my whiskey sour had nearly half melted. I picked up the glass and took a sip. "And where did you go after the divorce?" I asked.

"At first I didn't know where to go, so I went to a lot of places in the world. Geographically speaking, you could say I was running the whole time."

"So in the end, did you get sick of running too? Or did you realize your running was only running away from reality, and that's why you came back here to open a surf inn?"

He was quiet for a while, then asked, "You really don't have plans later?"

I shook my head and looked out at the cloud-packed sky. "I have all the time in the world. I'm all ears." And then he told me this story.

## 3

(For ease of telling, I'll relate his story in the first person.)

It happened the second winter after I left my wife and children. Before that I'd spent a year living in several countries in Europe. I made some good friends and called on a few writers I'd worshipped as a student. I spent my days reading, drinking the local wine and swimming. My love of swimming and diving had come back, for no particular reason, now that I was grown. Then I decided to see South America. I planned to go from Colombia all the way south to Argentina and see everything along the way.

First I spent three weeks in Bogotá and Medellín. Apart from some petty theft, nothing worth mentioning happened. Then I flew to Santa Marta, a very small city on Colombia's northeast Caribbean coast, between the mountains and the sea. I stayed in town for a few days, then decided to go hiking and camping in the hills nearby. I left most of my luggage at the hotel, took food and water, changed some cash, and set off. It was the dry season, so there were few mosquitoes. The air in the forest was dry and cool, the tropical trees rose into the sky, and the sound of the waves came through the gaps in the far-off trees. I'd set out in the afternoon, and when I reached the camp in the hills two-odd hours later it was evening. The sun was half down, the sunset spectacular. The camp sat on a huge, wide lawn in the middle of the forest, with a few tall palms scattered around, rows of tents for rent and a few big thatched huts. I'd sweated through everything on the walk up. Any other time I'd have stayed to watch the sunset, but just then all I wanted was to sit down and rest. I went into the thatched hut with the sign that said "Reception" to rent a tent. Inside was a wooden counter, like a bar. The last of the light came slanting in, and the boy behind the counter stood up and greeted me with a huge smile.

I have often wondered since whether the fate that tangles two people together is already decided the moment they meet. Every element essential, every past choice pushing you toward the one moment you both exist in. If I hadn't decided to come to Santa Marta; if I'd decided to hike in a different range; if I'd decided to walk one more kilometer to the next camp; or if I'd simply decided to watch the sunset a little longer and walk into that hut after the sun had set, would none of what followed have had to happen? But unfortunately, I can never escape fate.

The setting sun was in my eyes, and only when I reached the counter could I see what the boy looked like. Tall and lean, broad-shouldered, very young, with the light brown skin and brown eyes of people from there. Even now I can clearly remember that the first time I saw his eyes, something in my nerves seemed to get pricked. In my life I've seen so many pairs of eyes. Deliberate ones, wary ones, unruffled ones, gloating ones, ambitious ones. It has nothing to do with age or sex; eyes are born the way they are. His were unlike any eyes I'd ever seen. Not mocking, not arrogant, not giddy, not flattering, not dogmatic. They were the most open, the purest, the most unguarded eyes I have ever seen. Like the sea back home on a windless day in early autumn, stretching on forever without a speck in it. Like the long ranges of mountains. Like the moon at night. As it turned out, even calling them eternal wouldn't be too much.

My Spanish was halting, but luckily his English was good. He took down my details, took my money, and showed me around the camp. The smaller thatched hut was the dining room; dinner started at six-thirty. The even smaller one beside it was the shop, with local beer. The bigger hut farther off was the bathroom. Last of all he told me his name was Kenny, and if I had any questions I could find him in this hut or in the tent with the red top. I went back to my tent, put down my pack, took off my hiking boots, and fell asleep without meaning to.

When I woke it was nearly eight. It was completely dark and a little chilly. I threw on a jacket and walked over to the dining room, my stomach growling. I bought chicken and rice, then went next door and bought half a pack of local beer. There were a few hikers in the dining room, mostly in twos and threes. I found an empty table in the corner and sat down. I'd barely sat down when Kenny appeared from somewhere and sat across from me, smiling as hugely as before, with his own plate of chicken and rice. I gave him a beer and we got talking. He'd just turned nineteen, born and raised in the little town these hills belonged to, and hadn't stayed in school long. The camp was his grandfather's. In the dry season he came up to help, and in the rainy season he went back to town and worked at a restaurant. Most of his family did the same. That was how I learned that the woman selling beer in the little shop was his mother, the girl taking orders was his younger sister, the cooks were his father and his uncle, and everyone else working at the camp, at least ten of them, were relatives of one kind or another. What a family, I thought, amazed.

Kenny asked about my life, and I told him all of it. I said I was from a city a lot like Santa Marta, also very small, also between the mountains and the sea. After college I went to work in New York, then quit my job, got divorced, and now I was traveling. He wasn't interested in the rest, but he was very interested in New York. He asked all sorts of things: how cold the winters got, why the taxis were yellow, how many floors the tallest building had. He'd been born and raised here and had always lived here. The only place he'd ever traveled was a little island to the north called Puerto Rico, which was great for surfing; his parents had taken him for his fifteenth birthday. Surfing was Kenny's favorite thing in the world, and his dream was to open a surf inn someday. I asked if there was anywhere good to surf nearby. He nodded and said there was a beach with especially good waves an hour or two's walk away; he'd been that very morning. Then he grinned and said if I was free the next few days, he could take me. I said yes on the spot.

I'm no great surfer, and I don't love it the way I love swimming, but I grew up by the sea, so I'm at home with that sort of thing. For the next few days, after breakfast, Kenny and I went down to the beach. On the way there and back he asked a lot more questions, mostly about places I'd been. He said a lot of Dutch people had come through the camp, and he'd heard that in Holland there were enormous windmills and beautiful tulips and everyone rode a bicycle. He told me a lot about himself, too. His oldest brother had left home years ago to make his way in the world and now had a jewelry shop in Bogotá, the capital. His sister had married the man who lived less than a ten-minute walk from their house, the older brother of Kenny's best friend growing up; he ran a restaurant in town, and that was where Kenny worked in the rainy season. The friend had always been brilliant at school and was now studying medicine at the best university in the country. And at the end, shyly, he mentioned the girl he loved. They'd grown up together, childhood sweethearts, and he was going to marry her soon.

The mountain path was narrow, so he mostly walked ahead of me, and when there were rocks to climb he'd reach back and pull me up. He bounced along the whole way, his long limbs loose and easy, and when he wasn't talking he hummed local songs, brimming like the sea when the earth was born, holding every hope and every possibility. Sunlight fell through the gaps in the giant tropical leaves onto the dirt path, and the morning breeze came off the distant sea in gusts. Kenny's voice still had a teenage boy's crispness, like stepping on dry leaves in early autumn.

Only then did I realize why something in my nerves had been pricked when I first saw him. I envied him, hopelessly.

I don't think I had ever envied anyone before I met him. It sounds arrogant, but it's true. Of course there were plenty of people more handsome than me, richer, smarter, more powerful, more eloquent, with better trading strategies. Some people were shining when I was slinking home in disgrace; some were free when my life wasn't mine to run. But I never envied a single one of them. Maybe I envied my wife for having grabbed hold of something in life, envied my college girlfriend the sincerity of her love, envied a pianist in some jazz bar for how lost he was in his own playing. But I never envied any of them as a whole person.

But Kenny was different. He was one who didn't want to run, and I was one who did. And yet, somehow, he reminded me of who I used to be. Or more precisely, he stood for another possible life of mine. He'd grown up in the town where he was born and would probably live there for the rest of his life, marry, have lots of children, and his children would have lots of children. He knew there were many other possibilities in life, but he didn't wear himself out trying to run into one of them. He must have looked at the wide sea alone too, at the long mountains, at the moon overhead, but he'd never felt that nameless helplessness, nausea and fear the way I had. I watched the care in his face when he talked about his family, the sweetness when he talked about his girl, the longing when he talked about his surf inn. I watched how curious he was about other places, and yet how content he was with everything he had, how at ease, how happy. The thought of running had probably never even entered his head. And I kept thinking: if my DNA had been rearranged just slightly, would I have turned out not to be this person who wants to run, who has spent half his life running? Could I, like Kenny, have floated easily on the flood of fate and of the times, instead of straining so hard to grab hold of something?

The waves were great those days. I'm getting on in years and couldn't keep up, so I'd come in to rest after a short while. I sat on the sand watching Kenny rise and fall in the water and listening to the birds chattering in the trees. Now and then tourists walked by; young women in bikinis took pictures of each other in the shallows. The sun was brilliant, and far out the sea and sky melted into one color. And in a daze, a thought flickered through me: "Maybe now, I can stop running too?"

On my fifth day at the camp, Kenny died.

It was a weekend, and a lot of guests had come up to the camp, so Kenny was busy looking after them and we didn't go to the beach. Around six or seven in the evening I ate with Kenny as usual, and a couple who'd arrived that day sat down next to us. They'd been diving that afternoon at a beach nearby and were talking about the beautiful reef. That beach was about a thirty-minute walk from camp, smaller than the one Kenny and I usually went to, too rocky for surfing but very good for diving. Hearing that, I suddenly wanted to do a night dive. It was a full moon that night, very bright, and the day had been fine; there wasn't a cloud in the night sky. Kenny wanted to come with me. He said it was easy to get lost at night, and it would be much safer with him leading the way. Nothing was out of the ordinary on the way there. The forest at night was a little menacing, true, but the moonlight was clear. When we reached the beach it was only around eight. The wind off the sea was a little stronger, but nothing to worry about.

Kenny waited for me on the shore. Night diving is completely different from diving by day. The moonlight filters into the water, and the soft beam of the dive light cuts through the endless black of the sea floor. The silver sand on the bottom glitters, and the branching soft corals look even more delicate.

But it wasn't long before I felt something was wrong. A heavy shadow rose from below, the current turned violent and chaotic, and visibility dropped sharply. I looked up, and raindrops were hammering furiously on the surface above me. Only then did I realize the storm had come. I surfaced. Kenny was running into the shallows, shouting desperately for me to come back. I hadn't gone far; swimming back shouldn't have been hard. But my leg cramped, and I couldn't get any strength into it. I swam for the shore as hard as I could with the two arms I still had, but the sea that had been so calm and gentle by day was, for some reason, in a rage, and a single wave shoved me back. I tried to find the direction of the shore again, but the waves were enormous. I couldn't see anything but black water, couldn't hear anything but its roar. I shouted for help, but the moment I opened my mouth I swallowed several mouthfuls of seawater and wanted to throw up. Nothing I did helped. A huge helplessness took hold of me. My mind went blank. There wasn't even fear. This was my judgment day, and I was only the sea's puppet.

I closed my eyes and waited to die.

Then Kenny's hand gripped my arm, hard. He pulled me with one hand and swam forward with the other, fighting. The waves came one after another, harder and harder, and he broke through every one. I held on to Kenny with my other hand for dear life, praying for both of us. The beach got closer and closer; my feet were about to touch the sand. Suddenly a huge wave came surging in. Kenny yanked me forward and then gave me one hard shove. I stumbled and crawled up onto the beach. Even then I thought Kenny was right behind me. Coughing, I turned to look for him, and I watched the wave carry Kenny off and push him farther and farther out until he was a small dot in the distance, and then the endless black swallowed him.

It was as if a nerve in my head had snapped. The signals that should have traveled along it scattered in a panic, and the current they gave off numbed every part of my body. I couldn't move. I sat slumped on the sand. All my blood rushed to my heart, and there was a sweet, metallic taste in my mouth, as if it was about to come pouring out.

Kenny was pulled out of the water the next day by a fishing boat. The funeral was held in the town where he was born and raised. His enormous family came from everywhere: his parents, his little sister, his fiancée, the brother with the jewelry shop in Bogotá, the childhood friend studying medicine, his older sister and her husband. They all came. They shouted at me in words I couldn't understand, and sobbed in front of me and farther off. His mother, the short, plump woman who'd sold me beer with a smile, ran at me crying and beat me with her fists, and when people pulled her away she collapsed on the ground and stayed there. His father stood in a corner smoking the local cigarettes one after another, hunched over, coughing, wiping away tears with his head down. His little sister, the girl who helped in the kitchen and had asked me if I wanted chicken or beef, couldn't have been more than ten, and she stood behind her father staring at me. His fiancée was a girl who looked even younger than Kenny. Her beautiful eyes had cried themselves dry, her lips were white, and she leaned blankly against the coffin, her small body curled into a ball.

The stray dogs by the road, tongues hanging out, wouldn't come near me. The wind blew harder where I stood. Even the leaves on the trees trembled. I knew everyone wished it had been me they'd pulled out of the sea. Even I wished it had been me. At least my family wouldn't grieve like this. At least I was the one who wanted to run in the first place. I just stood there, frozen, head down, staring at my filthy hiking boots. I couldn't not go. I couldn't not stand there. I couldn't, at a moment like that, grandly announce that I wanted to run. After all, Kenny, the person who least wanted to run, had already run off to another world in my place. What an irony.

After the funeral I went back to the hotel where I'd left my luggage. I have no memory of how I got there. In fact, for a long time after that I couldn't take in anything happening around me. There was nothing left in my world but me, and Kenny, dead.

I sat in the hotel courtyard for a day and a night. There were a lot of towering trees in that courtyard, like the trees at Kenny's camp. People spoke to me, and I couldn't say a word. When it got dark I kept looking at the moon, and the moon didn't move either; it looked back at me from the sky. Not one cloud, not one drop of rain, and still that storm had to come on that day of all days.

And then I understood. My meeting Kenny, Kenny meeting me, my being saved by him, it was all destined, a huge metaphor fate had thrown at me, and a ringing slap across the face while it was at it. The thing you've searched for so hard, the thing you hoped to hold on to, the thing you hoped would bring you eternal peace, is running itself! There is no possibility in your life except running! You thought meeting Kenny meant meeting another possible you? That you could escape the life I wrote for you? Dream on! Open your eyes and look. You sent him to his death with your own hands!

And then I wept. I hadn't cried watching the waves swallow Kenny. I hadn't cried walking back through that forest to the camp like a ghost, or when I got there and told them what had happened, or when the people at the camp wept. I hadn't cried that night when I didn't sleep and prayed for Kenny, or the next day when I saw his swollen body. I didn't even cry at the funeral. But under that same clear moonlight I wept like a deranged monster, as if I had to use up every tear I'd stored up for decades.

Only then did I understand that in my whole goddamn life, nothing precious had ever been taken from me. Family, love, responsibility, duty, passion, all those precious things, every gift fate ever gave me, I gave up on every one of them before anyone could take it away. Only Kenny. Only Kenny was different. Only Kenny was taken from me while I was defenseless. I never even had a chance to fight.

I'm getting old. My hair is turning white, my eyes are getting worse, and still fate won't let me off. It never forgets to gloat and remind me: don't forget your mission in life. It's printed right there in your factory specs. You're someone who will always run. Don't go dreaming of turning into someone else halfway!

But I'm going to fight it. I wanted so badly to die and be done with it, to jump into that sea myself, to be swallowed by those waves and become a skeleton on the sea floor. But I couldn't. Death is the final running away, and I refuse to run anymore. I'm going to keep a part of Kenny, keep it inside me, and live in his place.

I canceled every travel plan I had, left Kenny's family a very large sum of money, and came here. I came to Kenny's favorite surfing beach and opened this surf inn. I surf every day. My body can't keep up, but I surf every day. Only when I'm in the sea do I feel Kenny is still alive. It's been five years now. I have never once left this place.

## 4

By the time he finished, it was evening. The man in front of me was sunk into the sofa, head bowed, suddenly much older, as if telling the story had used up all his strength. The rain had stopped and the clouds had thinned. Over the sea rose a sunset of the dazzling kind that only comes after a storm, as beautiful as a piece of music. I took a few cubes from the ice bucket and poured him a little more.

"So why sell this place after all?" I handed him the glass, and after some hesitation broke the silence.

But he didn't answer. He only shook his head. He picked up his glasses from the side table, took out the cloth and wiped them, then put them on. He stood, smoothed the wrinkles at the hem of his blue shirt, and said, "It's getting late. I'm going to turn in." And with that he left.

The lobby had emptied out. Most people had gone to the pavilion or the beach to watch the sunset, or to the restaurants nearby for dinner. I sat in that empty lobby with one thought I couldn't shake: Ian was obviously still in enormous pain. He hadn't been emotional telling the story. Even when he got to Kenny's death, though his voice rose a little, his face barely changed. But something in his eyes was being held down hard, something that seemed desperate to crawl out of his body. What was it? Was he still blaming himself for Kenny's death? Or did he regret the choices he had made in his life? I turned it over for a long time and found no answer.

I was starving, though. All afternoon I'd had nothing but that beer and the cashews. I was craving the lime-cured ceviche at the restaurant down the street, so I went upstairs to put my book away and went out to eat. That night there was another storm. I fell asleep to the wind and rain outside the window, and I had a dream.

I dreamed I was naked, shut inside a perfect, flawless glass case. My vision narrowed, I couldn't focus, I tried to get up and run, but my body wouldn't take orders from my brain. My sense of time was scrambled too. I don't know whether a second or a day passed before I could finally move my hand to pound on the glass, but nothing answered. The people outside the glass were well dressed and elegant. The men looked composed, in fine wool or camel-hair coats; the women were slender and dainty, carrying expensive designer bags. They stood around the glass case in a cluster, leaning in close to look at me, their eyes shining, whispering now and then to the person beside them, their lips moving in small, elegant motions. Several times I tried to wake, so that halfway between sleep and waking I could confirm the difference between dream and reality and go back to sleep in peace, but it was no use. In the dream I had completely forgotten the real world. All I knew was that I was a beautiful, very old exhibit, and that I couldn't get out of this glass cage. But the most frightening thing wasn't that I couldn't escape. It was that I had no idea how, or why, I had been shut inside the glass. I had no sense of cause or effect, no ability to reason about the facts, and my memories of myself were blank. I didn't even know who I was.

I woke drenched in sweat, still shaken. I stared at the ceiling for a long time, barely daring to breathe, confirming reality, afraid that if I let my guard down for a second I'd slip back into the world of the dream. Outside the window was the familiar beach, brightly colored paintings hung on the walls, and the air was warm and humid.

I let out a long breath and shook my head hard. The weather was fine, the sun bright. I got out of bed, washed up, and meant to go down, find him, and tell him about my strange dream. But I looked everywhere and couldn't find him. I asked the girl at the front desk; she said she hadn't seen him yet today. I ran upstairs and knocked on his door. No one answered, but through the door I could faintly hear music. I simply turned the handle. To my surprise, it wasn't locked; it opened at a push.

His room faced the beach, like mine. Sunlight spilled across the floor, and the whole room glowed. From somewhere, "Méditation" was playing again. The vibrato was just right, the shifts down the fingerboard smooth and perfect. But he wasn't there.

The bed was neatly made, the desk tidy and bare. The bookshelf was stacked with books, and I went along them one by one. One of them was South of the Border, West of the Sun. On reflex I pulled it out. It was very old, obviously read many times. Something had been tucked inside. At first I thought it was a bookmark, but then I saw it was a folded note. On the outside were a few words: "For the bookworm."

I unfolded the note. There were only three short lines, the handwriting flying across the page, as if scrawled in a hurry:

> "Thank you for being my audience.
> I wanted so badly to become myself, but for all my circling I still don't know what my real self is.
> I give up the fight. Whether I'm a warrior, or a coward."

~ (The End)
