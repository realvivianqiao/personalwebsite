I knew you for exactly one summer. It began at midsummer, when the city crews started repainting the railings along the coast road, and it ended when that long, grinding job was finally done. One summer, whole, with a clean line at either end.

We met the night our high school entrance exams ended. We were fourteen. I was grinding up a hill on my bike when you pulled up beside me on a brand-new carbon road bike, the bars wrapped in fresh sky-blue tape, and grinned over at me. "Brutal hill, right?" I was too out of breath to do more than nod. It was the stretch of the coast road I hated most, a long rolling climb that never seemed to end: push hard and it felt pointless, take it easy and it felt hopeless. "You have to find the right pace," you said. "Not too fast, not too slow. That's the trick."

We gave up and walked our bikes the rest of the way, and it turned out we went to the same school. Your grades were terrible and you didn't care, because your parents had already arranged for you to go abroad. Mine were good, and I was the one lying awake, afraid I'd choke and miss the honors class at the best high school. I should have seen then, alongside you, how absurd the world I believed in was. I didn't. Instead, for no reason I could have given, we agreed to ride together every morning that summer and, when we got tired, to lie on the beach and daydream. I had no interest back then in genetics or evolution or the long march of human history, and it never occurred to me to question a world that ran on short-sighted ambition and test scores. But I remember everything you said, and everything you believed. We'd lie on soft sand the tourists hadn't found yet, and you'd hold your hands up against the noon glare, half your face turned toward me. "All these genes, handed down for so long, and they decided we'd be here together, right now. Isn't that strange?" Or: "It took us six million years to walk out of Africa and another million to cover the whole earth. Say everyone only lived to fifty, and that's still a hundred and forty thousand generations of ancestors. Can you believe that?"

The summer you existed was the only summer we had. The sun was fierce, the sea glittered far out, the sand burned underfoot. Where you were going was only a twelve-hour flight away. But at the end of that summer you let go of the handlebars, went over the railing of the coast road and straight into the sea, and left me alone with every hard question this world has, to turn over and feel my way through by myself.

I have never found the right word for the time I had with you. I'm twenty-five now, not old, but a long way past fifteen, which is where you stopped. These ten years I seem to have marched boldly forward and marched in place at the same time. I think of you often, mostly in winter, the season we never got to share. This winter I went to the Galápagos; it took several connecting flights to get there. No one set foot on those islands until a little over four hundred years ago, and biologists call the animals there "incurably tame." Forty thousand years ago, the first people to reach Australia and New Guinea used that same incurable tameness to wipe out the marsupial lions and the rest of the giant mammals.

"Incurably tame." No other words would do. For a long time after you were gone, I blamed myself. If I had known anything besides classical Chinese, English vocabulary and quadratic equations, I kept thinking, maybe I could have kept you. If I could write poetry, I'd have written you poems to make the hardest heart cry. If I could sing, I'd have sung you your favorite songs and matched every note to the light in your eyes. If I could play the guitar, I'd have written you a song of your own, every tremor of the strings in time with your lashes in the wind off the sea. If I could paint, I'd have drawn you in the loose sand: half your face sinking into the blue sky, the sea behind you blazing in the sun.

If I'd been good at anything, I could have given you all of it. If I'd only done something differently, I might have caught hold of you and kept you here with something bright and rare. But my poems were clumsy, my singing off-key, my drawings crude. All I did was ride the coast road in circles and lie on the beach beside you, doing nothing.

It took me years to understand that even if I'd had those gifts, I wouldn't have used them. In all our time together, and I know the irony, I never once felt afraid of the future or of how big the world was. I never wanted to fight anything. I never noticed the hunter's gun at the back of your head. I was incurably tame. I did everything I was supposed to do: let off the brakes on the downhill and let the wind roar past my face, picked up shells on a beach where the only sound was cicadas, let the hours slip away on the climbs and the descents. Faced with what was coming for us, I was as tame as a dodo, standing there, curious, watching the hunters come ashore, and then watching them shoot you. Even if I'd known everything there was to know, understood all you understood and felt everything you felt, I couldn't have kept you. I couldn't have saved you.

And really, what would I have saved you from? Set beside any moment you were alive, everything the real world has to offer looks dull and shabby and not worth the trouble.

You were real. It's the world that's fake. I've thought that so many times since you left. People walked out of Africa, started wars and ended them, built towers on the ruins and watched them fall, and a few million years later here we are. And what is everyone working so hard for? Easy pleasures that take no thought. Good food and sweet nothings. A turn in the spotlight. A dress one size smaller, a bag one size bigger. More paper in the bank. A higher branch. Pleasing whoever gives the orders, so that one day they can give the orders. Making more little people. Keeping the great machine running smoothly, helping decide how its gears bite into its belts, they and their little people. Making sure they aren't the unlucky one.

I live in the city you were supposed to come to. Here some people wear beautiful suits and watches that cost more than a year of a child's school fees, and the school fees cost more than a car. Others stand in the cold outside a food pantry with their small children, waiting for dinner, or lie on a blanket on the sidewalk with a little dog in their arms, asking for change. Every morning I walk through all of them to my cubicle, to do a job neither of us knew existed when we were fifteen. On the last night of that summer we lay on the beach watching the black sea, calm except for the odd ripple, and you told me I was clever and worked hard and had a great future ahead of me. What great future did you mean? I hate this grind, and yet, like Lu Xun's Kong Yiji, I can't bring myself to take off the scholar's gown. Without the grind, what would I have to trade in this great machine?

How am I supposed to believe a world like this is real? How could I take anything from a world made of paper and lay it on the sea where you are, in the world where you are? How could I be sure that even if I had saved you, you'd have had a better life?

The truth, the real truth, is that I'm being selfish. If I could have kept you, I wouldn't have had to struggle so hard to understand how the world works or what life is supposed to mean. I could have slipped the fate written out for me, dodged the curse our genes put on us, and simply gone on, by luck, living in the same world as you. And in that world, a world with you in it, whatever its laws were, and whether it was real or fake, we'd have found the meaning of our lives just in having each other.

What a lovely life that would have been. What a simple one.
