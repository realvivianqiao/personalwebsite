That afternoon I thought of her again. The third decade of the twenty-first century was almost over, and she was still doing her best to dodge every branch and twig of this electronic jungle; she even handwrote her wedding invitations. I used to tease her: living like a hermit in this day and age, half the world has probably already declared you socially dead. She never minded. She'd blink those pale eyes under their thin lids and say, "As long as I'm still alive in your world."

That night, for the first time in my life, I got a phone call from the police. When I put the phone down, she had vanished from my world too.

The white invitation she sent was still lying quietly on the coffee table in my living room, mixed in with a pile of opened junk mail and overdue bills. "To my dearest front-row audience: I'm getting married! You have to come to the wedding!" Tucked inside was a photo of her and her fiancé against a deep, deep blue sea, both of them smiling brilliantly. The man's hair gel was slicked right down to the roots, and the watch on the wrist of the arm around her flashed in the Mediterranean sun, his wealth standing as tall as his hair. She wore a collarless lake-blue dress, her hair still twisted up in a bun, a necklace of colored stones around her long neck, and she was as beautiful as ever, beautiful enough to leave you speechless. What a mismatched pair, I thought when I saw the photo. And yet what a perfect match: a young, beautiful dancer and a potbellied real estate developer. The sweet fairy tale everyone loves to tell in the age of capitalism.

From the day the invitation arrived I had been racking my brain over who to bring as my date. On the day of her grand wedding, the role I needed to play was the old friend she hadn't seen in years, who had somehow, without anyone noticing, settled into a happy, stable life. Even if my real life was in pieces and my cheeks were hollow from drinking, she didn't need to know any of that. The best way was to have a date, someone to play the partner with me, the kind of part the camera sweeps past in a movie, happy and sweet, with nothing to say but a few pleasantries. In the adult world, having a steady partner seems to prove automatically that your life is going fine. She couldn't be too dazzling, and her acting couldn't be too clumsy; she couldn't make the audience too curious. An ordinary person you'd forget at a glance would be best.

In the end I settled on a young woman I had met not long before at my tennis club. Not ugly, not beautiful, not heavy, not thin, not loud, not quiet. Just right. But just as I was about to text the tennis girl, a call came in from a number I didn't know. The voice said it was the police, that I was her third emergency contact, that she had passed away.

When I think back on that call, it still strikes me as dramatically ironic. Whether she existed or not was announced by a single phone call from the police. If I had happened to miss the call that day, if I had been lucky enough to keep missing calls and then, by some fluke, miss the wedding too, maybe I would never have known she no longer existed.

I have kept wondering since what it really is that decides whether a person exists. If a person's social self disappears, if he cuts every social tie and goes to live alone in a cabin deep in the mountains without telling anyone, then to the people who knew him, is he alive or dead? Either, it seems. When other people withdraw from my life, I can assume they have fallen into an endless void, or I can believe they are doing well in some corner of the world. In the world where I am the subject, my imagination simply defines their existence, and in that sense, how convenient it is for me that they have vanished without a word.

But even if a person is still living in the spotlight, keeping up every tie to society, is his existence necessarily real? If, for most people, the form in which this person exists is his thoughts, and the form his thoughts take is writing, then if he were quietly murdered and his murderer took over his thoughts and kept on writing, would anyone know he no longer existed? And the pop star, the gifted trader, the teenage literary prodigy, the brilliant young founder, the eloquent politician, any of them could be only an empty shell, and under the shell, lines of code and lies designed by whoever makes the rules. History can be rewritten, facts can be twisted, and someone who never existed can live a dazzling life in village legend. The existence of great people gets abstracted into a spirit or a symbol, which lets them go on living after death. The existence of ordinary people is a fleck of spit; in the time it takes to laugh, a whole life is gone to ash and smoke. This fear of no longer existing, or rather of no longer existing in other people's worlds the way we imagine ourselves, might it explain how many people spare no effort building an image of themselves, proving their existence and their worth in other people's eyes?

That call came a month before her wedding, and three years after we had last seen each other. In those three years we had barely been in touch. We never called, never wrote, didn't even have any friends in common to tell me what kind of life she was living. I only thought of her now and then: her proud, beautiful face the first time I saw her outside a theater on a rainy night, the way she danced, drunk and happy, down an empty street at three in the morning.

Then her invitation arrived. If all the other mail hadn't been doing its best to grab my attention with flashy packaging, I wouldn't even have noticed her plain white envelope. The wedding was to be held at a resort her fiancé had just developed on some island. As soon as the news got out, the island became the place to be in the eyes of this city's potbellied rich men and young, pretty girls.

Every gossip account had something to say about the relationship, and her life was condensed into a few simple words, "young beauty," "young dancer," "gold digger," and dismissed. Only a few women racking their brains over how to marry rich dug up her entire history, down to the hour of her birth, and finally announced their conclusions with great authority: she had schemed to get close to so-and-so from the very beginning, and her such-and-such technique in relationships was worth learning from. Hardly anyone mentioned her name. Hardly anyone mentioned how she shone on stage. Hardly anyone mentioned that she loved to dance even when no one was clapping. Hardly anyone knew that she had never existed to satisfy anyone else's judgment. Hardly anyone knew who she was after the curtain fell.

Even on the day of her funeral, under a sullen sky with the north wind moaning, I still couldn't believe that the small wooden box held her. The therapist said denial is a normal, immediate defense mechanism after something sudden. But for me, if I never admitted she didn't exist, then she would still be dancing somewhere in the world, getting married, having children, growing old. I could still hold on to the hope of seeing her again someday, of sitting in her audience and clapping and cheering for her. I could still go with her after the curtain fell to the little shop on the corner and split a big box of pizza that had been reheated over and over. I couldn't stop wanting to make the mourners stop crying. Their tears were so weak, and mixed with the rain they made this reality more real and more absurd. I wanted to take out a bottle of hair gel and comb her fiancé's ridiculous hair, soaked by the rain and plastered limp against his forehead. How I wished she would suddenly appear from far away and tell everyone she was only an ordinary person after all, more curious about who would come to her funeral than to her wedding, and that was why she had played this enormous joke.

But night fell, the crowd went home, and there was nothing left but the lonely cry of a single bird, and me, her front-row audience.
