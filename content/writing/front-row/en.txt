I thought of her again that afternoon. The 2020s were nearly over and she was still doing her best to stay out of the digital jungle, so stubbornly that she handwrote her wedding invitations. I used to tease her about it. Living like a hermit in this day and age, I'd say, half the world must think you're dead. She never minded. She'd blink those pale eyes under their thin lids and say, "As long as I'm still alive in your world."

That night, for the first time in my life, I got a call from the police. When I hung up, she had vanished from my world too.

The white invitation she sent was still lying on my coffee table, buried in a pile of opened junk mail and overdue bills. "To my dearest front-row audience: I'm getting married! You have to come!" Inside was a photo of her and her fiancé against a deep blue sea, both of them beaming. His hair was gelled to the roots, and the watch on the wrist of the arm around her flashed in the Mediterranean sun, his money standing as tall as his hair. She wore a collarless lake-blue dress, her hair still twisted up in a bun, a necklace of colored stones around her long neck, and she was as beautiful as ever, beautiful enough to leave you speechless. What a mismatched pair, I thought when I saw the photo. And what a perfect match: a young, beautiful dancer and a paunchy real estate developer, the fairy tale everyone in the age of capitalism loves to tell.

Ever since the invitation arrived I'd been agonizing over who to bring as my date. At her big wedding, my role would be the old friend she hadn't seen in years, who had quietly settled into a happy, stable life. My real life could be in pieces, my cheeks hollow from drinking; she didn't need to know. The best way was to bring a date, someone to play my partner, the kind of part a camera sweeps past in a movie, happy and sweet, with a few lines of small talk and nothing more. In the adult world, a steady partner seems to prove all by itself that your life is going fine. She couldn't be too dazzling or too bad an actress; she couldn't make anyone too curious. Someone ordinary and instantly forgettable would be best.

In the end I settled on a young woman I'd met recently at my tennis club. Not ugly, not beautiful, not fat, not thin, not loud, not quiet. Just right. But just as I was about to text her, a call came in from a number I didn't know. The voice on the line said it was the police, that I was her third emergency contact, that she had passed away.

When I think back to that call, it still strikes me as almost theatrically ironic. Whether she existed or not was announced in one phone call from the police. If I'd happened to miss the call that day, and been lucky enough to keep missing calls and somehow miss the wedding too, I might never have known she was gone.

I've wondered ever since what it really means for a person to exist. If someone's social self disappears, if he cuts every tie and goes to live alone in a cabin in the mountains without telling anyone, is he alive or dead to the people who knew him? Either, it seems. When people drift out of my life, I can assume they've fallen into some endless void, or I can believe they're doing well in some corner of the world. In a world where I'm the one doing the looking, my imagination simply decides whether they exist, and in that sense, how convenient it is for me when they disappear without a word.

But even if someone is still living in the spotlight, with every tie to the world intact, is he necessarily real? If, for most people, he exists as his ideas, and his ideas reach them as writing, then if he were quietly murdered and his murderer took over his ideas and kept writing, would anyone know he was gone? The pop star, the gifted trader, the teenage literary prodigy, the brilliant young founder, the silver-tongued politician: any of them could be an empty shell, and underneath it nothing but lines of code and lies designed by whoever makes the rules. History can be rewritten and facts twisted, and someone who never existed can live a dazzling life in village legend. Great people are abstracted into a spirit or a symbol and live on after they die. Ordinary people are a fleck of spit; their whole lives are gone in the time it takes to laugh. This fear of not existing anymore, or of not existing in other people's worlds the way we imagine ourselves, might it explain why so many people work so hard on their image, proving in other people's eyes that they exist and that they matter?

The call came a month before her wedding and three years after we'd last seen each other. In those three years we'd barely been in touch. We never called, never wrote, didn't even have friends in common to tell me how she was living. I only thought of her now and then: her proud, beautiful face the first time I saw her, outside a theater on a rainy night; the way she danced, drunk and happy, down an empty street at three in the morning.

Then her invitation came. If all the other mail hadn't been shouting for my attention in flashy packaging, I'd never have noticed her plain white envelope. The wedding was to be held at a resort her fiancé had just built on some island. The moment the news got out, the island became the place to be for every paunchy rich man and every young, pretty girl in the city.

The gossip accounts couldn't stop talking about them, and her life was boiled down to a few words, "young beauty," "dancer," "gold digger," and dismissed. Only a few women desperate to marry money dug up her whole history, down to the hour she was born, and solemnly announced their findings: she had schemed to get close to so-and-so from the start, and her such-and-such technique was worth studying. Hardly anyone used her name. Hardly anyone mentioned how she shone on stage. Hardly anyone mentioned that she loved to dance even when no one was clapping. Hardly anyone knew that she had never existed to please anyone's judgment, or who she was once the curtain came down.

Even on the day of her funeral, under a sullen sky with the north wind moaning, I couldn't believe that the small wooden box held her. The grief counselor said denial is a normal defense mechanism after a sudden shock. But as far as I was concerned, as long as I never admitted she was gone, she was still dancing somewhere in the world, getting married, having children, growing old. I could still hope that one day I'd see her again, sit in her audience, and cheer. I could still go with her after the curtain to the little place on the corner and split a big box of pizza reheated too many times. I couldn't stop wanting the mourners to stop crying. Their tears were so feeble, and mixed with the rain they made all of it more real and more absurd. I wanted to take out a bottle of hair gel and fix her fiancé's ridiculous hair, plastered limp to his forehead by the rain. I wished so badly that she'd suddenly appear and tell everyone she was only human after all, more curious about who'd come to her funeral than to her wedding, and that was why she'd played this enormous joke on us.

But night came, the crowd went home, and there was nothing left but the lonely call of a bird, and me, her front-row audience.
