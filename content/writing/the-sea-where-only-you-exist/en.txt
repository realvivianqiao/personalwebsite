I only knew you for one summer. It began at midsummer, when the city sanitation crews had just started repainting the railings along the coast road, and it ended at summer's end, when that whole sprawling project was finally finished after day upon day of hard labor. One whole summer, with clean edges.

The first time we met was the night the high school entrance exams ended. We were fourteen. I was pedaling hard up a hill, and you came up from behind on the newest carbon-fiber road bike, its handlebars wrapped in brand-new sky-blue tape. You turned your head, grinned, and said, "Tough hill, huh?" I was out of breath and could barely nod. Of the whole coast road, I hated that stretch most: an endless, rolling, gentle climb where giving it everything felt pointless and taking it slow felt hopeless. "You've got to find a pace that's in harmony, a perfect pace," you said. "Not too fast and not too slow. That's how you do it."

We got off and walked our bikes up the rest of that long hill together, and found out we happened to go to the same middle school. Your grades were terrible, but you didn't care in the least, because your parents had already arranged for you to study abroad. My grades were good, and I was the one sick with worry that I would choke and miss the honors class at the top high school. I should have realized then, along with you, how absurd the world I believed in was. But I didn't. Instead, for no reason I could name, I agreed that we would ride together every morning that summer, and when we got tired we would lie on the beach and daydream. Back then I had no interest at all in genetics or evolution or the course of human history, and not the slightest doubt that a shortsighted, utilitarian, results-driven society made sense. None of that kept me from remembering clearly what you said and what you believed. We lay on soft sand the tourists hadn't found yet, and you held up both hands to shade our eyes from the harsh noon sun, half your face turned toward me, and said, "Our genes have been passed down for so long, and they decided that right here, right now, we'd be together. Isn't that amazing?" And then: "It took humans six million years to walk out of Africa, and another million to cover the earth. Even if you say people live to fifty, to be conservative, that's a hundred and forty thousand generations of ancestors. How incredible is that?"

The summer you existed was the only summer we shared. The midsummer sun was scorching, the sea glittered in the distance, the sand baked hot. The place you were going was a place a twelve-hour flight could reach. But you had to let go of the handlebars as that summer ended, go over the railing of the coast road, and charge into the sea without looking back. You had to leave me alone, to keep turning over and groping through and facing all the hard questions of this world by myself—

How do I describe the time I had with you? I am always looking for the right word. I am twenty-five this year, which is not old, but next to you, stopped at fifteen, I have certainly aged. These ten years I seem to have marched forward with great swagger, and also to have marched in place. I think of you often, especially in winter, the winters we never got to meet in. Again and again I think of you. This winter I went to the Galápagos, which took several connecting flights. No human set foot on those islands until a little over four hundred years ago, and biologists describe the animals there as "incurably tame." Forty thousand years ago, the first humans to reach Australia and New Guinea used exactly that incurable tameness to wipe out the marsupial lions and the other giant mammals.

"Incurably tame." It seems to be the only right phrase. For a long time after you left I blamed myself, over and over. I kept thinking that if back then I had known anything besides classical Chinese, English vocabulary and quadratic functions, maybe I could have kept you here. If I could write poetry, I would have written you beautiful poems, so moving that the coldest heart would weep reading them. If I had a beautiful voice, I would have sung you your favorite songs, every rise and fall of every note matched to the light in your eyes. If I could play the guitar, I would have played you a piece written only for you, every tremble of the strings in time with your eyelashes trembling in the sea wind. If I could paint, I would have drawn in the loose sand the outline of half your face sinking into the blue sky, and the sea behind you shining in the sun.

If I had been good at something, I could have given you everything I could get my hands on. If I had done something different, it seems, I could have quietly pulled you back, kept you here with those shining things and precious moments. But all I had were clumsy poems, off-key songs, awkward drawings. All I did was ride in circles on the coast road, over and over, and lie on the beach beside you doing nothing at all.

It took me a long time to understand that even if I could have done those things, I couldn't have done them. Because in every moment with you, however ironic it seems now, I never once felt afraid of the future or of the enormous world, never once wanted to fight or rebel, never once noticed the hunter's gun pressed to the back of your head. I was incurably tame. I did everything I was supposed to do. I let off the brakes on the downhill and let the wind howl past my face. I picked up shells on the quiet beach where there was nothing but a few cicadas. I let time slip away on the climbs and the descents. Before your fate and mine, I was as tame as the flightless dodo, standing still and looking with curiosity at the humans arriving, then watching as you were shot. Even if I had known everything there is to know in this world, understood all that you understood, felt for all that you felt for, I couldn't have kept you. I couldn't have saved you.

Or, when it comes down to it, what could I have saved you from? Everything in the real world, set beside any moment you existed in, only looks dim, only looks too shabby to mention.

You were real, and this world is fake. In so many moments since you left, I have thought that over and over. Humans walked out of Africa, started wars, ended wars, built towers on the ruins, watched the towers fall, and after a few million years arrived at this world. What are the people of this world so busy and so empty for? For simple pleasures that don't require thinking. For food and drink and sweet talk. For the spotlight. For a dress one size smaller and a bag one size bigger. To save up more paper. To climb taller trees. To please the people who give them orders. So they can give orders to more people later. To make more little people. So that the great machine runs a little smoother. So that they and their little people can help design how its gears mesh with its belts. So they won't be the unlucky one.

Now I live in the city you were supposed to come to. In this city some people wear sharp suits and a watch that costs more than a year of a child's tuition, and the tuition costs more than a car. Some people stand in the cold wind outside a food pantry with their small children, waiting for dinner, or lie on a blanket on the sidewalk, begging, a little dog in their arms. Every day I walk through all of them on my way to my little cubicle, to do a job that, at fifteen, neither of us knew existed. On the last night of that last summer before you left, we lay on the beach looking at the black sea. The sea was calm, with an occasional ripple. You told me I was so smart and worked so hard, I would definitely have a great future. But what great future did you mean? I hate this busy emptiness, and yet, like Kong Yiji, I can't bring myself to take off the scholar's long gown. Without this busy emptiness, what could I trade for anything in this great machine?

How am I supposed to believe that a world like this is real? How dare I take anything from a world made of paper and offer it to the sea where you are, to the world where you are? How dare I say with certainty that even if I could have saved you, you would have had a better life?

When it comes down to it, when it really comes down to it, I am only thinking selfishly. If I could have kept you, I wouldn't have had to work so hard to make sense of how the world runs and what life truly means. I could have escaped the law fate laid down for me, dodged the curse our genes put on us, and simply gone on existing, by chance, in the same world as you. And in that world, a world with you in it, whatever its objective laws, even whether it was real or fake, we could have found the meaning of our lives just in the fact that the other was there.

How beautiful that life would have been. How simple.
