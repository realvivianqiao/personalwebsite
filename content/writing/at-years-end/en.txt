I'm not quite done being twenty-three, and 2022 has only a few days left. I always used to count Lunar New Year as the end of the year, but this time, when in Rome, December 31 gets to be the ending and the beginning. One by one, everyone I know is leaving New York for somewhere warm to wait out the winter. Work has slowed down. There are fewer people in suits rushing for the E train and more tourists happily posing for photos in Times Square and outside Rockefeller Center. The water outside my window has gone a few shades darker. The huge ships that docked all summer are gone, and Pier 83 sits empty and bleak, the lights on the water rippling now and then. There's a thin frost on the streets, and the doormen have switched to heavy coats and hide in the space between the two front doors.

I'm still happy some days and low on others, on top of the world one minute and sure I need to be doing more the next. Maybe it's the extra free time, but I've started thinking about things like who I am and where I came from and where I'm going, and I've gone back through Sartre and Saramago and Kundera and Dazai, the ones I'd read and the ones I always meant to. When I was a student, books and films were just entertainment; the ten thousand lives in them had nothing to do with me. Reading them now, it suddenly hit me that my life and everyone else's are only the lives in books, crossing and branching and running on, or that who I am right now is just a small portrait of one side of some character.

Kundera said his characters came from sudden flashes of inspiration in everyday life, which he then fleshed out until a person emerged. Like the specks of mud the goddess Nüwa flicked from her rope to make people. Those specks are Tereza torn between body and soul, between kitsch and grace. They're Yozo's forced smiles and put-on frivolity after he's seen everything the world has to show. They're the beautiful girl with the dark glasses, good and upright and rebellious all at once. They're Kazuko realizing in a single instant that she burns for Uehara. They're all those beautiful girls in Chinese TV dramas who peak too early, with heads in the clouds and hands that can't reach. They're Cat Power singing "The Greatest" under her breath. They're Tom, who dropped out of a PhD at Cornell to drive a cab in New York, with all his resentment and resignation.

But do we study the past to understand the present? Or is it that we can never know what to want, because we only live once and have nothing to compare it to? How easy empathy is, and how pointless.

I spent most of 2022 chasing things that mean something: work, the gym, love. And the last little stretch chasing things that don't mean much: drinking, romance, paintings, sunsets, and random city walks.

In high school I used to study on the Harbin Institute of Technology campus. One day I opened a notebook some student had left behind and found a little grumble inside: "My ordinary life, my ordinary love." I didn't get it at all back then. I thought love and ordinary had to be opposites, that however ordinary a life was, the moment love arrived it would turn extraordinary. Only later did I begin to see that, even now, two people don't always end up together because of love. It can start with practical calculations, with loneliness, with something unhealthy in the mind, with parents' earnest advice, with needing someone to lean on, rather than with one stunned glance across a crowded room.

People say falling in love is a process of disenchantment, but to me it's more like enchantment. Company and dependence are a kind of nourishment that slowly makes a relationship that maybe wasn't quite right, or was even fragile, feel normal. They're also a pot of warm water: soak in it long enough, and even when it tastes of nothing, it seems a waste to pour it out. "Love grows heavy once it's made public." And isn't that heaviness, that reluctance to pour it out, just the weight of the promises and responsibilities you've taken on?

With someone I like lately, I went to Central Park and sat on a rock where you can see Central Park Tower, looking at the black lake and guessing whether a giant sea monster might suddenly rise out of the water. We bought cheap bagged wine at CVS and drank as we walked, and every strange thing looked like a portal: an oddly wide clearing in a tangle of trees, a cartoon pig sticker on a traffic light, the window of a dollar-pizza place, the stairs from a roof deck up to the top floor, a red EXIT sign at the end of a black hallway. Lightness is always fun, but without your noticing it turns heavy, and once it's heavy it goes flat. Is romance romantic because it's light, or simply because it's romance? Or is lightness a prerequisite, so that the moment worldly ties come into it, you're back to calculating P&L and sunk costs and the relationship's rate of return?

The pain of being human isn't only anger at your own limits. More of it, maybe, comes from the gap between what you want and what you have, from the dithering and second-guessing that come from not being brave enough, from lying awake at night wondering, resentful and curious, about the road not taken. I want freedom and drifting, and I'm afraid of ending up alone on Su Shi's cold sandbar. I want calm and stability, and I long to be carefree and knocked about by the world. I want romance and that rush in the chest, and I'm afraid of lowering myself into the dust for someone. I want money and status, and I look down on the vulgar. I want art and philosophy, and I still have to tend my little plot in front of a screen and keep serving capitalism. So I end up stuck in between, swinging back and forth like a pendulum. But at all these forks in the road, who knows what the right choice is, the right combination? And what does right even mean?

At moments like this, no speck of mud can tell me the answer. I have to walk every road myself. The specks can explain my past, but they'll never tell me my future.

"We pass through this world like travelers on a long road." Next year, I want to live a little freer, a little lighter.
