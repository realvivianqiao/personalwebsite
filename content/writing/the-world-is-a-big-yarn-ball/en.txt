"The world is a big yarn ball." The line came back to him as he sat on the cold windowsill of a north-facing apartment on the Upper East Side. Behind him were the bare branches of the plane trees, old men in camel-hair hats moving slowly along the street, and a winter sun that could barely be bothered. The apartment was on the third floor, and every step up to it was a good four inches taller than a Chanel flap bag. The red paint on the banister was peeling, and the stairwell reeked of cheap Chinese takeout. The old prewar building seemed to have gone into hibernation thirty years early, listless, spending what little energy it had on refusing to be torn down or renovated and on keeping up those ridiculous stairs. Where did they lead? Not to a temple of knowledge or a heaven of truth or the answer to life. They led to a three-bedroom on Park Avenue listed at five million dollars, to the few afternoons of his childhood he'd spent with his mother, to the place his father, dying, had asked him to buy back.

His mother left when he was five. Soon after, the apartment was sold to a couple newly arrived from London. They lived here twenty-five years and were now selling to retire to Westchester.

The door was unlocked. He let himself in; the broker hadn't come yet. The furniture was gone and the rooms were empty, except for a bright red poinsettia in full bloom on the windowsill, all wrong in that grey room. The walls had been freshly painted, and the old window unit he remembered, always humming, was gone. The December wind battered the glass. There were no curtains, but there were hooks along the top of the window frame; the couple must have put them up. In his memory his mother had taken the hooks down one by one with her own hands. "A north-facing apartment gets no sun as it is. Curtains would only block what little there is." The vase was the wrong color, the frames were too stiff, the bracelet should have been gold, not silver: he couldn't remember how many times she and his father had fought over things like that. She always won in the end, and his father always came home with flowers.

The world is a big yarn ball. Out of nowhere, he remembered it. His mother had said it on a winter afternoon not long before she left. She hardly ever talked to him. Sometimes she'd take him onto her lap, her hair brushing the tip of his ear, and sing him "Somewhere Over the Rainbow." But mostly she sank into the wing chair with a glass of cabernet and an old record playing and stared at the plane tree outside the window.

He was on the thick, brightly patterned rug, patiently building with his blocks, and she was standing at the tall, narrow window, looking out for a long time. Then, out of nowhere, she said it. He looked up, confused. She turned, came over, bent down and touched his cheek, her beautiful eyes narrowing as she looked at him. "The world is a big yarn ball," she said, "in that you don't know how it began, how it will end, and why it's tangled this way."

He still remembers her eyes. He means that no matter whose eyes they'd been, he would have remembered them; they were the kind of eyes you can't forget. He had inherited them, so he knew what eyes like that could do. Add the faintest color to them and people would fall at your feet. But she couldn't be bothered with color. She could make the most mocking face, the angriest face, her features could twist and her voice could climb an octave, but her eyes stayed quiet, calm, almost indifferent.

She hated the north-facing windows so much, hated how little sun the apartment got, hated, he realized later, her family and her life. And yet that winter afternoon, sitting by his half-built castle and talking to him, haltingly, for longer than she ever had, there was no weariness or disgust in her eyes at all. There was nothing in them. He couldn't tell whether nothing could hurt her or nothing held her. Like a great king who chooses to freeze his own kingdom in ice. Like Baldr, whom the gods pelted with swords and shields and stones for sport.

"The world is a big yarn ball," he said under his breath. But how could it be? The rules of the world had been worked out long ago. Big bang, steady state, oscillating universe, however it began, it had to unfold in a line, the way Darwin said: natural selection, survival of the fittest. And besides Darwin, he had a hundred other ways to explain why society works the way it does. Nietzsche, Hegel, Adam Smith, and their thousands of disciples.

"Only the unlucky wonder about the meaning of life." His father had told him that all his life. "It's easy to be one of the lucky ones. All you have to do is listen to us." As the only son, he never disappointed anyone. Besides, as his grandfather said, being lucky wasn't hard. "Just don't do what your father did and marry some outrageous woman. Don't do anything that far out of line." Ahead of him lay a broad road already paved, the aunts and uncles he'd known since he was born, beauty queens with sparkling eyes he could glance right past. The price for all of it was obedience. Doing what he was told by everyone who would decide his life. He'd never minded. If he walked his life obediently, it came with a guarantee of smooth sailing and universal adoration. Why bother thinking, or running?

He shifted on the sill and opened the window. The December wind had been waiting, and it rushed in, bitterly cold.

The day his mother left had been this cold too. He came home from school and the nanny took him straight to the dining table. It was three days before Christmas Eve, and his favorite cherry pie was waiting. From the living room he heard his father's voice, shaking: "She's gone." Then his father crying: "She just left." He put down the cherry he'd just speared and looked up at the nanny, lost. She kept her eyes down and wiped her hands on her apron, glanced at him, and quickly looked away.

Against the advice of his financial advisors and his grandparents, his father sold the apartment and moved into a south-facing one his grandfather had just bought. He sat alone in front of the huge sunlit windows smoking cigars, and the apartment was as silent as a grave. He drank with his friends, opening bottle after bottle from the cellar, and his friends brought young, pretty girls, loud and shrill, their pretty eyes full of want and calculation. None of them had eyes like his mother's. "If we'd stayed in that apartment, would everything be different?" He heard his drunk father say it over and over. "If I'd picked the painting she liked, would she still have left?" The bottles piled up every night, and every morning the nanny cleared them away.

Before long his father laid down his arms entirely and did whatever his grandfather told him. He took the position his grandfather found for him, listened to his parents, walked the road paved for him, and rose without effort. The one and only rebellion of his life ended when she left. He still doesn't know whether his father truly made his peace with fate and with being one of the lucky ones, or whether he threw himself the other way only to convince himself that his earlier choice had been a mistake, that his rebellion had meant nothing.

His mother smoothed her skirt, sat down behind his castle, and bent her head to help him find the next block. Her hands were slender, her nails as red as the poinsettia, her wrists bare. In no time she found the piece he'd been hunting for, the one for the gatehouse pillar, and handed it to him.

"Sweetheart, the world is a big yarn ball. Do you understand? However smooth and perfect the outside is, the inside can be completely different. There are no rules. Every stitch looks as if it's following the pattern exactly, but really it can go any which way. You can wind the ball in the most efficient way there is, or the most complicated, mysterious way. Inside the ball there's no time. Everything in your life, what time calls past and present and future, is there all at once, with no before or after, just one possibility tangled up with the next. All you do is walk from one point in the ball to another, from one possibility to another." She paused, leaned over and stroked his hair. "If you're lucky enough, you'll get to walk to every point and try every possibility."

Without thinking, he snapped off the poinsettia blooming in its pot on the sill and crushed it between his fingers. Sticky white sap ran over his hand. With a poinsettia, the bewitching red isn't the petals, it's the leaves. Poisonous, though not deadly.

I thought I was lucky enough, he thought, grinding the last of the red leaves to pulp.

The world he'd grown up in had been a plain, straight story. He'd had no destiny, only a lot in life. Whatever he was born to be, he was, or was on his way to becoming. His beautiful, outrageous mother was just a footnote in his family's history and his own. When he saw the old Mexican man pushing his empanada cart through the freezing wind, the homeless man with his bed of flattened boxes, the girl in Central Park clutching a big sketchboard and crying in the shadow of a rock, the junior clerk in his cubicle agonizing over a promotion that never came, he always felt that whatever it was that swept other people along and forced them forward, the sandstorm or the flood or the tornado, had never so much as touched the banks of his life.

She found the blue pieces for the moat and handed them to him. He set the last one under the bridge, completing the moat, and looked up at her, glowing with pride.

She smiled, touched his cheek, and went on softly. "One day you'll reach the very middle of the ball, and you'll feel a kind of suffocation, like weightlessness, and a sickness you can't name. You'll want to retch and retch, you'll want to close your eyes and pretend to be asleep so you don't have to face it. You'll see some people strutting, some people scheming and scrabbling, some perfectly pleased with themselves. You'll see the tallest tower fall overnight and the most passionate young man go grey overnight. You'll understand that the deepest love is also the heaviest chain, and the most painful goodbye can bring the sweetest hope. In the darkest night you'll be blinded by sunlight, and on the brightest summer afternoon you'll feel cold to the bone. You'll find that even the homeless man living in a cardboard box puts on a red sweatshirt he found for Christmas. You'll find the world is full of contradictions, ridiculous, and there's nothing to be done about it. And most important of all, you'll find that the ball doesn't begin at the middle, and doesn't end there either. Its two ends could be hidden anywhere."

She stood up. "So, sweetheart, I hope you walk to every point in the ball. Only if you reach every point will you have been brave enough, and lucky enough."

That was the last thing he remembers her saying to him. Then she put on her long red coat, opened the door, and left.

The door swung open and the broker, all in grey, hurried in. He walked over, flicking away the crushed poinsettia, his fingers itching, and shook the broker's hand. "So sorry I'm late, sir." The broker offered some polite condolences on his father's death, then broke into a huge smile.

"Could I have a little time here on my own?" he said, cutting off the flood of words. "I've decided to buy it. Email me the details, or we can set up a time to go over them." The broker lit up with a joy he couldn't hide, said a few more nice things, and left in a hurry, as if afraid he might change his mind.

He shut the door and sat down on the floor in the spot where he'd built his castle at five. His grandfather had lost his power, his father was dead, his lover and his friends had turned and walked away. His castle had fallen before it was finished; at five he'd picked up the half-built thing and smashed it. He had no idea where the blue moat or the gate pillars had ended up. The castle he never finished. The goodbye he never said.

"Goodbyes and reunions should both have music," his father had told him before he died. "At the funeral, play 'The World We Knew.' It's the song we were listening to the night I met your mother."

He took out his phone and found the battery dead. His fingers stung and itched from the sap. In the dim room he finally couldn't hold it back, and he started to cry. "Do I have to feel sick before I can walk the whole ball? Do I have to walk all of it before I can be free? Do I have to be free before I can be lucky? Do I have to be lucky before you'll bless me?" His head was between his knees and his shoulders shook. "If you bless me, will I be safe from some stray sprig of mistletoe?"

The wind had dropped. There was hardly anyone in the street, and everything was as silent as an endless sea about to close over his stranded boat. The neighborhood his father had avoided all those years was as ordinary as a neighborhood can be. The towering plane trees, the red brick wall across the street, the grit glinting under the streetlights. He smoothed the creases from his long black coat, dried his beautiful eyes, and left without looking back.
