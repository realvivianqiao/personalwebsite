金色蹄钉

Golden Horseshoes

Golden Horseshoes

金色蹄钉

10 min read约 7 分钟First published on WeChat ↗首发于微信公众号 ↗

"有些人对自己的命运感到满足，有些人对自己的命运感到不满，但这并不重要。重要的是一个人如何承受自己的命运。"

— 阿尔贝·加缪，摘自《西西佛神话》

马不止一次地在同一个路口看见同一个人。拿鞭子的人总在这个路口停下招揽乘客，等待的空当它得以四处张望。路对面有一个高高的广告牌，上面是一匹比它神气得多也健壮的多的马 — 长长的马鬃整洁飘逸，马皮油亮没有一点鞭子抽打留下的疤痕；后蹄着地，两个前蹄抬起，蓄势待发之状，蹄子上镶嵌着亮闪闪的金色蹄钉。

对于马来说，让它记住一个人，着实比记住一个路口要难得多。那究竟从何时起，为甚么, 马的脑海里给这个人留了块空地，连马自己也不清楚.

马只记得在一个冬天的傍晚第一次注意到此人。天冷, 乘客和拿鞭子的人讨价还价，它不耐烦地抬了抬蹄子。夕阳落了一半，余晖照在积雪融化后脏兮兮的街道上; 顺带慷慨地兼顾广告牌下站着的那个人。马的视力不好，不知道在人类的世界里, 这人是美丽还是丑陋; 这人的神态是何，它也看不真切。但据它对人类这么多年的观察来看，这人太不起眼 — 如果和拿鞭子的人讨价还价，气焰多半会被狠狠压下去; 搞不好还要落得个哑口无言。

拿鞭子的人和乘客讨价还价的时候，马虽不参与，但也会在一旁默默地作出评判。马的心里有一台秤砣，秤砣总是倒向气势上更弱的人。倒不是出于什么道义上的考虑，马单纯希望这关于几枚硬币的讨论可以早点结束; 而只要有人气势弱下来，那就是白旗的预兆。讨论的结束意味着它又要拉着车和人在同一条路上重复，意味着又要被鞭子抽打。这马也清楚。无论是在路口等待还是在大路上前行，马都没有机会换上那金色的蹄钉。无非是一种无意义状态和另一种无意义状态之间的切换，不分优劣，本质相同，像是拿锋利的刀割水。但即便如此, 马还是愿意一遍又一遍地踏上同样的道路; 与其说是麻木, 不如说是释然。

马生长在河边的马厩里，拿鞭子的人换了一批又一批，身边的同伴也换了一匹又一匹。它起初还努力记住每个人和马的名字，直到和马一同长大的最后一匹马也死掉，它方觉白费力气才作罢。马也算饱经世事，阅历丰富，书当然是没读过，但故事是听了很多，路也走过不少。马印象最深刻的是那个神气的美国人和"绿灯"的故事。"绿灯有什么特别的?"后座的父亲给小姑娘讲这个故事的时候，马边听边想。"前面就是绿灯，这大路上随处可见绿灯。"

马之后在一年的不同季节里，又看见这个人好几次。有个炎热的夏天突然暴雨，拿鞭子的人抽它，催促它快些。它跑过那块广告牌的时候，又瞥见那个人撑伞站在广告牌下。马回到马厩，甩掉身上的雨珠，一个古怪的念头冒进它脑子里："这人不会是在等我吧？"拿鞭子的人拿来干草。马随即把这念头抛掷脑后。

马最后一次看见这人，是在来年冬天的深夜。马这时显然老了，肚子更瘦了，步子也迈地缓慢；但拿鞭子的人没有让它退休的意思。它又跑了一天，回到马厩。同伴大多睡了，它靠在墙上，低头默默咀嚼干草。

那人就在这时出现了。马先是听见越来越近，一重一轻的脚步声；然后一双金色的靴子停在了它的干草槽前。那靴子一尘不染，在路灯的光下反射出耀眼的光。马见过各种样子的靴子，浅口切尔西皮靴，中筒棉靴，过膝长靴; 它们踢在自己肚子上的力道各有不同. 马还不知道这崭新的金色靴子踢在它肚子上会是什么感觉; 它甚至都没有见过金色的靴子。倒也合乎常理, 无论是骑马还是坐马车, 都不要脏了鞋子才好; 金色是多么华而不实的颜色。

马抬头，才发觉是那个人。马是怎么知道此人即彼人呢？马也给不出一个完美的答案，但它唯一确信的是，和即使是初出茅庐的拿鞭子的人讨价还价能输的一败涂地的，除眼前人外别无他人了。此人形貌普通，实无可圈可点之处；背着黑色旅行包，套着灰色棉服。姑且判断是个即将秃顶的中年男性。脚上的金色靴子实在突兀，马向下看去，还是个瘸子。一条腿显著的细，一边的肩在厚厚的棉服下显著的低。

男人突然开口: "我可以解放你"。声音尖而细, 像是变声器没结束的男孩的声音: "我看你在这城市奔波太久，实在于心不忍；我没能力做更大的事情，但至少想让你在老死之前可以不用再被鞭子抽打。"

"像你这样的马，不应该被束缚在马厩之中，供人载客，受鞭挞之苦; 甚至尾巴被绑上皮绳参加赛马, 都是对你的不敬之举。"男人继续说道："你应该在广袤的草原上自由奔跑，或者至少在这条大路上自由驰骋。"

马只不过是普通的比利时马, 深知自己没什么高贵的血统, 或者是过人的本领. 自己对男人为何如此特殊? 马不敢轻易上当, 毕竟它是见过世面的马。八成又是一个想要救赎它的人, 一个想救鱼的假慈悲的猴子. 这种人马见过不少 — 无非是想通过拯救它而被命运拯救, 想通过歌颂它而被世界歌颂。说到底他们和拿鞭子的人一样，并不关心马想要什么, 只想通过它实现自己想要的。不对, 甚至比拿鞭子的人更糟糕, 他们不但通过它实现自己想要的, 还假惺惺作态, 假装关心马想要的; 实则胡乱塞给马些什么后便离去, 自私地感到一种圆梦的快感。但这男人也是其中一员吗?

马长长睫毛下的眼睛盯着男人。男人胡子稀疏，五官圆钝，脸也苍白, 头发快掉光了, 实在是一张没什么男子汉气概的脸; 但小小的灰色眼睛里似乎有着真挚的关切。

马开口："金色的蹄钉，不要灰色的"。

男人愣了一下, 重复道："金色的蹄钉？"

马点点头，"我只要金色的蹄钉就够了"。

男人没说话, 退后了几步, 戴上棉服的帽子, 转身离开了; 背影一瘸一拐。马叹了口气，气从鼻孔里逃出来。马继续低头吃草，仔细地品味没什么滋味的干草。同伴们都睡得很熟，他们还都值壮年，是可以吃好睡好第二天继续卖力干活不用思考任何事情的年纪。

马清楚地知道自己的命运。像它之前说的，无非是等待载客和载客两种状态之间的切换。即使在广阔的草原上自由地奔跑，那不过也只是拥有了第三种状态 — 是一池水汇入了另一池水，归根到底也还是无意义的状态, 脚下也还是灰旧生锈的蹄铁. 马不想要自由, 它都不知道自由是什么; 它只想要金色的蹄钉, 想成为广告牌上那匹神气的马.

但出乎马的意料, 男人回来了。他气喘吁吁跑回来的时候，马不知道过了多久。夜色更粘稠，空气更凛冽。男人苍白的脸因为奔跑有了血色，本就不多的头发因为出汗粘在脑门上。他手里握着的东西金灿灿的。他拿着四个金色蹄钉。

它们和那广告招牌上的金色蹄钉一模一样，甚至更美。只有真正的金子才会发出那种细腻温柔而又脆弱夺目的光.

男人翻身钻进马厩，小心翼翼地避免碰到它的同伴。他麻利地拔下马陈旧生锈的蹄铁，换上新的。男人说自己小时候就瘸腿，所幸可以骑马；在马背上长大，还参加过不少比赛。马默默听着，看放在泥土和粪便上被更换下来的马蹄铁。这肮脏东西居然在自己身体里存在了这么多年。

男人摸了摸马的鬃毛, 说了句再见; 翻身离开马厩, 又看了它一眼. 转身离开了. 这是马最后一次看见男人.

第二天的电视新闻上，男人看见了马。"马惊险举蹄，三名乘客轻伤"。马夫惊魂未定, 但因难得有摄像机对准自己而难掩声音里的兴奋："这老马性格温顺，载人几十年了从来没出过事儿；今儿个不知怎么了，突然在这个路口受了惊，不受控制地站起来开始叫唤。"

镜头切换到远处行人抓拍的照片。马虽然老瘦，脏，棕色地皮毛上难掩鞭子留下的疤痕，但神态举止和后面的广告招牌一模一样 — 后蹄着地，两个前蹄抬起，蓄势待发之状，蹄子上是亮闪闪的金色蹄钉。马车的乘客花容失色，前一分钟为了几枚硬币发起的争执早就忘到了九霄云外。还没等警察暴力解决马就倒下了，从它在空中到倒下不过五秒。画面切换到巨大的挖掘机，正试图铲起运走马的尸体。

新闻草草结束; 一匹马的死亡倒还新鲜，如果是人怕是连电视都上不了。接着的是果汁广告，颜色鲜艳，台词空洞, 音乐单调又愉悦，一派快活。鲜嫩多汁的草莓和蓝莓，穿着干净粉色衬衫的漂亮演员，世界上所有的战争，痛苦，挣扎都不存在。"品味自然的精华，每滴果汁都是生命的拥抱！"

男人不知道该开心还是难过。他究竟是个救赎者, 还是个旁观者, 他也无从知晓.

"Some people are content with their fate and some are not, but that doesn't matter. What matters is how a person bears it."

— Albert Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus

More than once, the horse saw the same person at the same corner. The man with the whip always stopped at this corner to drum up passengers, and while they waited the horse got to look around. Across the street was a tall billboard, and on it was a horse far more splendid and far sturdier than this one. Its long mane was sleek and flowing, its coat glossy, without a single scar from the whip. Its hind hooves were planted and its forelegs raised, poised to spring, and its hooves were set with gleaming golden horseshoes.

For a horse, remembering a person is a good deal harder than remembering a corner. So when, and why, the horse had cleared a little space in its head for this person, even the horse couldn't say.

All the horse remembered was first noticing this person one winter evening. It was cold, a passenger was haggling with the man with the whip, and the horse lifted a hoof impatiently. The sun was half down, and its last light fell on the filthy street where the snow had melted, and generously took in the person standing under the billboard as well. The horse's eyesight was poor. It couldn't tell whether, by human standards, this person was beautiful or ugly, and it couldn't make out the person's expression either. But going by its years of watching humans, this one was far too forgettable. If this person tried haggling with the man with the whip, they would most likely be crushed, and quite possibly left speechless.

When the man with the whip haggled with passengers, the horse took no part, but it judged in silence from the side. In the horse's heart was a balance scale, and the scale always tipped toward whoever was weaker. Not out of any moral consideration. The horse simply hoped the discussion about a few coins would end sooner, and the moment someone weakened, that was the white flag coming up. The end of the discussion meant pulling the carriage and its people down the same road again, and being whipped again. The horse knew this too. Whether waiting at the corner or moving along the avenue, the horse would never get the chance to wear those golden horseshoes. It was only a switch from one meaningless state to another, neither better nor worse, the same in essence, like cutting water with a sharp knife. And even so, the horse was willing to set out on the same road again and again. Less numbness, you might say, than release.

The horse had grown up in a stable by the river. The men with the whip changed, one batch after another, and the companions beside it changed, one horse after another. At first it tried hard to remember every person's name and every horse's name, until the last horse it had grown up with died too, and only then did it realize the effort was wasted and give up. The horse had seen a good deal of the world and lived through a good deal. It had never read a book, of course, but it had heard a great many stories and walked a great many roads. The one that made the deepest impression was the story of the dashing American and the green light. "What's so special about a green light?" the horse wondered, listening, as a father in the back seat told the story to his little girl. "There's a green light right up ahead. This avenue is full of green lights."

After that the horse saw this person several more times, in different seasons of the year. One hot summer day a sudden downpour came, and the man with the whip lashed the horse to make it hurry. As it ran past the billboard it glimpsed the person again, standing under the billboard with an umbrella. When the horse got back to the stable and shook the rain off itself, a strange thought popped into its head: "Could that person be waiting for me?" The man with the whip brought hay. The horse put the thought out of its mind at once.

The last time the horse saw this person was late one night, the following winter. By then the horse was plainly old. Its belly was thinner, its stride slow, but the man with the whip had no intention of retiring it. It had run another full day and come back to the stable. Most of the others were asleep. It leaned against the wall, head down, quietly chewing its hay.

That was when the person appeared. First the horse heard footsteps coming closer, one heavy and one light, and then a pair of golden boots stopped in front of its hay trough. The boots were spotless and threw back a dazzling light under the streetlamp. The horse had seen all kinds of boots, low-cut Chelsea boots, mid-calf padded boots, boots that came over the knee, and they each kicked its belly with a different force. The horse didn't yet know what these brand-new golden boots would feel like kicking its belly. It had never even seen golden boots. That made sense, though. Whether you're riding a horse or riding in a carriage, you don't want to get your shoes dirty, and gold is such a showy, impractical color.

The horse looked up, and only then did it see that it was that person. How did the horse know this person was that person? The horse couldn't give a perfect answer either, but the one thing it was sure of was that no one but the person in front of it could lose so completely in a haggle with even the greenest man with a whip. This person was ordinary-looking, with truly nothing remarkable about them, carrying a black travel bag and wearing a grey padded coat. A middle-aged man, the horse decided, about to go bald. The golden boots on his feet were jarring. The horse looked down and saw that he was lame, too. One leg was noticeably thinner, and under the thick padded coat one shoulder sat noticeably lower.

The man spoke all of a sudden. "I can set you free." His voice was high and thin, like a boy's whose voice hasn't finished breaking. "I've watched you working this city too long, and I can't bear it. I'm not able to do anything bigger, but I'd at least like you not to have to feel the whip again before you die of old age."

"A horse like you shouldn't be tied up in a stable, carrying passengers, suffering the lash. Even tying your tail up with a leather cord and putting you in a race would be an insult to you," the man went on. "You should be running free across the open grasslands, or at least galloping free down this avenue."

The horse was only an ordinary Belgian draft horse. It knew perfectly well it had no noble bloodline, no extraordinary gifts. Why would it be so special to this man? The horse wasn't about to be taken in so easily; it was a horse that had seen the world, after all. Most likely this was another one who wanted to redeem it, another monkey full of false compassion, pulling a fish out of the water to save it from drowning. The horse had seen plenty of people like that. All they wanted was to be saved by fate by saving the horse, to be praised by the world by praising the horse. When it came down to it they were the same as the man with the whip. They didn't care what the horse wanted; they only wanted to get what they wanted through it. No, they were worse than the man with the whip. Not only did they get what they wanted through it, they put on a show of pretending to care what the horse wanted, and then shoved something at it and left, selfishly enjoying the thrill of a dream come true. But was this man one of them?

The horse's eyes, under their long lashes, studied the man. His beard was sparse, his features soft and round, his face pale, his hair almost gone. It really wasn't much of a manly face. But in his small grey eyes there seemed to be genuine concern.

The horse spoke. "Golden horseshoes. Not grey ones."

The man was taken aback. He repeated, "Golden horseshoes?"

The horse nodded. "Golden horseshoes are all I want."

The man said nothing. He stepped back a few paces, pulled up the hood of his padded coat, and turned and left, limping away. The horse sighed, the breath escaping through its nostrils. It lowered its head and went on eating, carefully tasting the tasteless hay. The others were all sound asleep. They were still in their prime, the age when you can eat well and sleep well and work hard again the next day without having to think about anything at all.

The horse knew its fate clearly. As it had said before, it was only a switch between two states, waiting to carry passengers and carrying passengers. Even running free across the open grasslands would only mean having a third state, one pool of water flowing into another, meaningless all the same in the end, with the same old grey rusted shoes on its hooves. The horse didn't want freedom; it didn't even know what freedom was. All it wanted was golden horseshoes. It wanted to be the splendid horse on the billboard.

But to the horse's surprise, the man came back. When he came running back, out of breath, the horse had no idea how much time had passed. The night was thicker, the air colder. The man's pale face had color in it from running, and what little hair he had was stuck to his forehead with sweat. Something in his hands shone gold. He was holding four golden horseshoes.

They were exactly like the golden horseshoes on the billboard, more beautiful, even. Only real gold gives off that kind of light, delicate and soft and yet fragile and blinding.

The man swung himself into the stable, careful not to bump the other horses. Deftly he pulled off the horse's old rusted shoes and put on the new ones. He said he'd been lame since he was a boy, but luckily he could ride. He'd grown up on horseback and even ridden in quite a few competitions. The horse listened in silence, looking at the old shoes lying in the mud and manure where they'd been dropped. To think that filthy stuff had been part of its body for so many years.

The man stroked the horse's mane and said goodbye. He climbed out of the stable, looked at the horse once more, and turned and left. That was the last time the horse saw the man.

On the television news the next day, the man saw the horse. "Horse rears in terrifying incident, three passengers lightly injured." The carriage driver was still shaken, but with a camera pointed at him for once, he couldn't keep the excitement out of his voice. "This old horse is as gentle as they come. Carried people for decades and never had a single incident. I don't know what got into it today. It just spooked at this corner, out of nowhere, reared up and started screaming, and there was no controlling it."

The shot cut to a photo a passerby had snapped from a distance. The horse was old and thin and dirty, its brown coat unable to hide the scars of the whip, but its bearing was exactly that of the billboard behind it: hind hooves planted, forelegs raised, poised to spring, and on its hooves the gleaming golden horseshoes. The carriage passengers had gone white with fright, and the argument over a few coins a minute earlier was long forgotten. Before the police could deal with it by force, the horse collapsed. From rearing into the air to falling, it took no more than five seconds. The picture cut to a huge excavator trying to scoop up the horse's body and haul it away.

The news wrapped up quickly. The death of a horse was still something of a novelty; if it had been a person, it probably wouldn't even have made television. What came next was an ad for juice, bright colors, empty lines, music monotonous and cheerful, everything joyful. Plump, juicy strawberries and blueberries, a pretty actor in a clean pink shirt, and all the wars and pain and struggle in the world did not exist. "Taste the essence of nature. Every drop is life's embrace!"

The man didn't know whether to be happy or sad. Whether he was a redeemer or a bystander, he had no way of knowing.
