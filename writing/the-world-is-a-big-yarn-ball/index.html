The World Is a Big Yarn Ball

这个世界是一个巨大的毛线球

12 min read约 8 分钟First published on WeChat ↗首发于微信公众号 ↗

"一切都淹没在自己的无限中，然后，我拼命从那无限中逃脱出来，尽管我被这个无限淹没."

"这个世界是一个巨大的毛线球", 他想起这句话的时候, 正坐在上东区北向房子的冰冷窗沿上. 背后是悬铃木光秃的枝干, 戴着驼毛帽子行动迟缓的老人, 和消极怠工的冬日阳光. 房间在三楼, 通向这个房间的每一节台阶都比Chanel CF高至少十个公分, 扶手的红色油漆剥落, 楼道里弥漫着不合格的中餐外卖刺鼻而辛辣的味道. 这个战前的老旧公寓, 像是在三十年前就已经提早进入冬眠. 有气无力; 所有的精力都用来了拒绝拆迁和翻新, 和维护这些高的离谱的台阶. 这些台阶通向哪里? 好像也不是知识的殿堂, 真理的天堂, 或是人生的答案, 是这个Park Ave上售价五百万美金的三居室, 童年记忆里和妈妈度过的为数不多的时光, 父亲临终前让他买回的地方.

妈妈离开时他五岁. 房子接着就被转卖给了一对从伦敦刚搬来纽约的夫妻, 他们在这儿住了二十五年, 又决定将房子卖掉, 搬到Westchester养老.

门没锁, 他推门进去, 中介还没来. 家具都已经被搬走, 空空荡荡. 只有窗台上一盆盛开的鲜红的Poinsettia, 和这个灰蒙蒙的房间格格不入. 墙壁刷了新漆, 记忆里老旧嗡嗡作响的箱式空调也已经不在. 纽约十二月的寒风呼啸着击打着窗户, 窗户没有窗帘, 但上沿有窗帘挂钩, 应该是之后的夫妇安装上去的. 记忆里是妈妈亲手把挂钩一个一个拆下来. "北向的房子本来就没有阳光, 再有窗帘岂不是遮挡更多. " 诸如此类的事情 — 花瓶的颜色不对, 画框的设计太过呆板，手镯是金色不是银色, 他不记得为此她和爸爸吵过多少次架. 但她最后总是战胜, 爸爸总是送花道歉.

"The world is a big yarn ball." 他突然没由来地就想起这句话. 这是妈妈走前的那个冬日午后对他说的. 妈妈几乎不和他说话; 只是有时会把他抱在怀里, 发丝触着他的耳尖, 给他唱"Somewhere Over the Rainbow". 但大多的时候, 她陷在高背沙发椅里, 手里拿一杯赤霞珠, 放着一张老唱片; 就那么定定地看着窗外那棵悬铃木.

他坐在图案绚烂的厚地毯上耐心地拼积木, 她站在狭长的窗前久久凝视着窗外. 妈妈没由来的这么一句. 他迷茫地抬起头, 妈妈转过身, 走到他身前, 俯下身, 摸了摸他的脸颊; 漂亮的眼睛眯起来, 盯着他, 又继续说道: "The world is a big yarn ball, in that you don't know how it began, how it will end, and why it's tangled this way."

他到现在都记得那双眼睛.他的意思是, 无论这是谁的眼睛, 他都会记得, 那是双看一眼就忘不掉的眼睛. 作为她遗传因子的完美继承者, 他理所当然地知道这么漂亮的眼睛的威力 — 只要稍加一点色彩, 就会有无数人跌倒在这个陷阱里. 可是她不屑于那色彩. 她可以做出最戏谑和最愤怒的表情, 面部可以扭曲, 声音可以提高八度, 可是那双眼睛总是安静的, 波澜不惊的, 甚至是淡漠的.

即使她那么讨厌北向的窗户, 讨厌这房间稀少的阳光, 或者在他之后想来, 那么的讨厌她的家庭, 她的人生, 在那个冬日的午后, 当她坐在他搭建了一半的积木城堡前, 第一次和他断断续续说了那么多话的时候, 他一点也没有从她眼睛里看到倦怠和厌恶. 她的眼睛里什么都没有, 不知道是因为没有什么可以伤害她, 还是没有什么可以让她牵挂. 像是伟大的王选择冰封他的国, 或者是被众神扔剑、扔盾牌、扔石头的巴德尔.

"The world is a big yarn ball", 他轻声重复了一遍. 可怎么会是呢? 这个世界的规律分明早已经被解释清楚. 无论是大爆炸的, 静态的, 还是震荡的, 无论它是怎么开始的, 演化的过程总该是线性的, 达尔文的, "物竞天择, 适者生存". 当然除了达尔文, 他还有一百种方式去解释这个社会运行的原因 — 尼采, 黑格尔, 亚当斯密, 以及他们的千百个信徒.

"只有不幸者才会思考人生的意义", 这是从小到大爸爸重复给他的人生哲理, "你可以很简单的做一个幸运的人; 只需要听我们的话." 他作为独子, 理所当然不负众望. 更何况, 像爷爷说的，做一个幸运的人, 这本身就不是件难事 — "只要别像你爸爸那样，和离谱的女人结婚，做这种太出格的事情就好". 前面是铺好的康庄大道, 是从小就熟识的叔叔阿姨, 是可以一扫而过的选美小姐亮晶晶的眼睛. 当然一切的代价, 是听话. 听话, 听所有会决定他人生的人的话.倒也没有什么问题.毕竟他的人生, 如果听话的走过, 是被打了保票的顺风顺水, 和万众宠爱; 那又何苦要去思考和逃离?

他在窗沿上侧过身, 打开窗户, 十二月的寒风早已等候多时，急不可耐地闯进来, 甚是凛冽.

母亲离开的那天, 也这么冷. 他放学回家, 阿姨直接把他带到了餐桌. 还有三天就是平安夜了, 桌上有他最爱的樱桃派. 他隐约听到客厅爸爸颤抖的声音 — "她走了", 然后是爸爸的抽泣, "她就这么离开了". 他放下刚叉起的樱桃, 茫然地抬头看阿姨. 阿姨眼睛低垂, 在围裙上擦擦手, 看向他后又迅速移开.

后来便是爸爸不听投资顾问和爷爷奶奶的劝阻, 卖了这个房子, 移居到爷爷刚购置的南向房子. 爸爸坐在巨大的阳光明媚的落地窗前一个人抽雪茄, 房子是安静的死寂. 和朋友们一起喝酒, 把酒柜里的酒开了一瓶又一瓶; 他的朋友们带来年轻漂亮的女孩.她们喧哗而又聒噪, 漂亮的眼睛里全是欲望和算计, 没有一个人的眼睛像妈妈. "我们当时如果选择住这个房子, 是不是一切就都不一样? " 他听见醉酒的爸爸一遍又一遍地重复, "如果我挑的是她喜欢的画, 她是不是就不会离开? " 酒瓶总是堆一晚, 第二天早上就被阿姨收拾走.

再没过多久，爸爸对他的命运彻底缴械投降，对爷爷唯命是从。他接受了爷爷安排的职位，听爷爷奶奶的话，走在早已给他铺好的路上，平步青云轻而易举。他人生中唯一一次和最后一次的反叛，随着妈妈的离开落幕。他到现在都不知道，爸爸是真的接受了命运，接受了做一个"幸运的人"；还是说他非要走上相反的道路，只是为了说服自己相信，之前的选择是错误的，之前的反叛是无意义的。

妈妈捋了捋半身裙, 坐在他的城堡后面, 低头帮他找下一块积木. 她的手纤细, 指甲和Poinsettia一样鲜红, 手腕干净. 她很快就找到他苦苦寻找的应该放在门廊柱子上的积木, 递给他.

"宝贝, 这个世界是一个巨大的毛线球, 你明白吗? 球的外面再光滑完美，里面都可以大不相同。它没有什么规律, 每一个针脚的走向都看似一丝不苟遵循规律, 实则可以是完全胡乱的编织. 你可以用最efficient的方式织这个球, 也可以用最繁复, 让人捉摸不透的方式织这个球. 时间在毛线球里并不存在，你人生的所有 — 按照时间的观点所说的过去现在和未来，都同时存在。他们没有先后，只是交织在一起的一种可能和另一种可能。你只是从毛线球的一个点，走到另一个点；从一个可能移到了另一个可能。"她顿了一下, 探身摸摸他的头发, 继续说道："如果你足够幸运 — 那你就可以走遍所有的点，尝试所有的可能性。"

他无意识地掐断，继而捻碎窗台上花瓶里盛开的Poinsettia, 粘腻的白色汁水沾满了一手. Poinsettia，妖艳的红不是花瓣，而是叶子；有毒，但不是剧毒. "我以为我已经足够幸运了"。他想，捻碎手里残留的红色叶片.

他迄今为止长大的世界, 是平铺直叙的; 他没有命运, 只有命. 他生下来应该是什么, 他就是什么; 或者说是在成为"是什么"的道路上。他那美丽而出格的母亲, 只不过是他的家族，他的人生微不足道的一个小插曲.

他看到凛冽寒风里推小推车卖Empanada的墨西哥老人，废纸箱搭成临时床铺的流浪汉，抱着大画板在中央公园石头阴暗面抽泣的女孩，在格子间苦恼久久不能升职的小职员，就总是觉得，那些所有裹挟着别人生命使他们不得不前进的东西-沙尘暴, 洪流或者是龙卷风 — 好像从来就没有触碰过他生命的河岸.

她又找到了组成城堡护城河的蓝色积木, 递给他. 他接过蓝色积木, 放在桥下，那是护城河的最后一片. 他一脸骄傲地抬起头看妈妈.

她笑着看他, 摸了摸他的脸颊, 又轻声继续说道: "总有一天，你会走到毛线球的最深处，感到一种失重般的窒息感和一种莫名的恶心。你会想要长时间的干呕，想要闭上眼睛假寐来逃避。你会看到一些人耀武扬威，一些人蝇营狗苟，一些人自得其乐; 你会看到最高的楼在一夜之间坍塌，最热情澎湃的年轻人在一夜之内白头；你会明白最深的爱也是最重的羁绊，最痛苦的离别却能带来最甜蜜的期待；你会在最深的黑夜里被刺眼的阳光迷了眼睛，在最明亮的夏日午后感受到彻骨的寒意; 你会发现在纸箱里安居的流浪汉也会在圣诞节换上捡来的鲜红套头卫衣. 你会发现这个世界充满矛盾，滑稽可笑; 而又无可奈何。最重要的是，你会发现，这个毛线球并不是在最深处开始，也不是在最深处结束。它的起尾点可能藏在这个球体的任何一处. "

她站起身："所以我说，宝贝, 我希望你能走遍这个毛线球的所有点. 如果你能走遍所有的点，那你才是足够勇敢，足够幸运。"

那是记忆里妈妈对他说过的最后一句话。说罢，她便穿上红色的长外套，推开门走了。

门突然被推开, 一身灰色的中介匆忙走进. 他走向门口, 弹走手里的Poinsettia, 手指发痒. 和中介握手寒暄. "不好意思先生, 我来晚了."礼节性地对他父亲的去世表示抱歉, 随即堆了满脸笑容.

"我可以一个人在这里呆会儿吗?" 他打断中介的滔滔不绝. " 我已经决定买下了. 具体信息邮件发给我就好, 或者我们也可以约时间再详谈". 中介的脸突然被点亮, 难以掩盖住的欣喜若狂; 客气了几句就匆匆离开, 仿佛是担心他随时会反悔.

他把门关紧, 席地而坐, 坐在五岁的他拼积木的地方. 爷爷失势, 父亲去世, 爱人和朋友转身就走开. 他的城堡还没有拼完, 就已经倒塌; 五岁的他把那个半成品举起摔碎. 蓝色的护城河, 城墙的门柱, 早就不知道被扔在了哪里. 他无法完成的城堡，他未曾说出口的告别。

"离别和重逢, 都还是要有音乐的. " 父亲临终前和他说. "葬礼的时候, 就放The world we knew, 是你妈妈和我第一次遇见的时候听的歌".

他掏出手机, 才发现手机没电了. 手指因为花汁过敏痒的生疼. 昏暗的房间里，他终于忍不住地哭了起来, "非要感到恶心, 我才能走遍这个毛线球吗? 非要走遍, 我才能自由吗? 非要自由, 我才能幸运吗? 非要幸运, 我才能被你祝福吗? " 他的头埋在膝盖之间, 肩膀控制不住地颤抖, " 如果被你祝福, 我就不会被一支没由来的槲寄生刺死吗?"

风停了, 街道上行人少的可怜, 四周静默的像一片无垠的海, 要把他这条搁浅的船裹挟吞噬.父亲生前避之唯恐不及这么多年的街区, 平凡的不能再平凡. 参天的悬铃木, 对面红色的砖墙, 在路灯下闪烁的石砾. 他抚平黑色长大衣上的褶皱, 擦干漂亮眼睛里的泪. 头也不回的离开了.

"Everything drowns in its own infinity, and then I fight my way out of that infinity, even as it drowns me."

"The world is a big yarn ball." He remembered the words while sitting on the cold windowsill of a north-facing apartment on the Upper East Side. Behind him were the bare branches of the plane trees, old men in camel-hair hats moving slowly, and winter sunlight that could barely be bothered to show up. The apartment was on the third floor, and every step leading up to it was at least ten centimeters taller than a Chanel Classic Flap. The red paint on the banister was peeling, and the stairwell was thick with the sharp, spicy smell of bad Chinese takeout. This old prewar building seemed to have gone into hibernation thirty years early. Listless, every bit of its energy spent on refusing to be torn down or renovated, and on keeping up these absurdly tall stairs. Where did these stairs lead? Not, it seemed, to a temple of knowledge, a heaven of truth, or the answer to life. They led to this three-bedroom on Park Avenue listed at five million dollars, to the few stretches of time in his childhood he had spent with his mother, to the place his father asked him, before he died, to buy back.

His mother left when he was five. The apartment was sold soon after to a couple who had just moved to New York from London. They lived here for twenty-five years, then decided to sell and retire to Westchester.

The door was unlocked. He pushed it open and went in; the broker hadn't arrived. The furniture had all been moved out, and the place was empty. There was only a pot of bright red poinsettia in full bloom on the windowsill, out of place in that grey room. The walls had been freshly painted, and the old, humming window unit he remembered was gone. December wind howled and battered the windows. There were no curtains, but there were curtain hooks along the top, which the couple must have put up later. In his memory it was his mother who had taken the hooks down, one by one, with her own hands. "A north-facing apartment gets no sun to begin with. Curtains would only block more." Things like that, the vase was the wrong color, the frames were too stiff, the bracelet should be gold, not silver; he couldn't remember how many times she and his father had fought over them. But in the end she always won, and his father always brought flowers to apologize.

"The world is a big yarn ball." Out of nowhere, he remembered the line. His mother had said it to him on a winter afternoon just before she left. His mother hardly ever talked to him. Sometimes she would hold him in her arms, her hair brushing the tip of his ear, and sing "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" to him. But most of the time she sank into the high-backed armchair with a glass of cabernet, an old record playing, and simply stared at the plane tree outside the window.

He was sitting on the thick, brightly patterned rug, patiently building with his blocks, and she was standing at the long, narrow window, gazing out for a long time. Then, out of nowhere, she said it. He looked up, puzzled. She turned, came over to him, bent down and touched his cheek, her beautiful eyes narrowing as she looked at him, and went on: "The world is a big yarn ball, in that you don't know how it began, how it will end, and why it's tangled this way."

He still remembers those eyes. What he means is, no matter whose eyes they were, he would have remembered them; they were the kind of eyes you can't forget after one look. As the perfect heir to her genes, he naturally knew the power of eyes that beautiful. Add the slightest bit of color to them and countless people would fall straight into the trap. But she disdained that color. She could make the most mocking face, the angriest face, her features could twist, her voice could jump an octave, but those eyes were always quiet, unruffled, even indifferent.

Even though she hated the north-facing windows so much, hated how little sun the room got, or, as he came to think later, hated her family and her life so much, on that winter afternoon, when she sat in front of his half-built block castle and, for the first time, talked to him in fits and starts for so long, he saw no weariness or disgust in her eyes at all. There was nothing in her eyes. He didn't know whether it was because nothing could hurt her, or because there was nothing she cared about. Like a great king who chooses to freeze his own kingdom in ice. Or like Baldr, whom the gods pelted with swords and shields and stones.

"The world is a big yarn ball," he repeated softly. But how could it be? The laws of this world had long since been explained. Whether it began with a big bang, or was static, or oscillating, however it began, its evolution had to be linear, Darwinian: natural selection, survival of the fittest. And besides Darwin, he had a hundred other ways to explain why society runs the way it does. Nietzsche, Hegel, Adam Smith, and their thousands of disciples.

"Only the unlucky think about the meaning of life." That was the philosophy his father had repeated to him all his life. "You can very easily be one of the lucky ones. All you have to do is listen to us." As an only son, he of course lived up to everyone's hopes. And besides, as his grandfather said, being one of the lucky ones wasn't hard in itself. "Just don't do what your father did, marrying some outrageous woman, doing something that far out of line." Ahead of him was a broad, smooth road already paved: the uncles and aunts he had known since he was little, the sparkling eyes of beauty queens he could glance past. The price of all of it, of course, was obedience. Obedience to everyone who would decide his life. There was nothing wrong with that, really. After all, his life, if he walked it obediently, came with a guarantee of smooth sailing and universal adoration. So why bother thinking, or running away?

He shifted on the windowsill and opened the window. The December wind had been waiting a long time and burst in impatiently, very cold.

The day his mother left had been this cold too. He came home from school and the nanny took him straight to the dining table. It was three days before Christmas Eve, and his favorite cherry pie was on the table. Faintly he heard his father's shaking voice from the living room, "She's gone," and then his father sobbing, "She just left." He put down the cherry he had just speared with his fork and looked up at the nanny, bewildered. The nanny kept her eyes down and wiped her hands on her apron, glanced at him, and quickly looked away.

After that, against the advice of his financial advisors and his grandparents, his father sold this apartment and moved into a south-facing one his grandfather had just bought. His father sat alone in front of the huge, sunlit floor-to-ceiling windows smoking cigars, and the apartment was silent as death. He drank with his friends, opening bottle after bottle from the wine cabinet, and his friends brought young, pretty girls. They were loud and shrill, their pretty eyes full of want and calculation, and not one of them had eyes like his mother's. "If we'd chosen to live in that apartment back then, would everything be different?" He heard his drunk father say it again and again. "If I'd picked the painting she liked, would she not have left?" The bottles piled up every night, and every morning the nanny cleared them away.

Not long after that, his father laid down his arms before fate completely and did whatever his grandfather told him. He took the position his grandfather arranged, listened to his grandparents, walked the road already paved for him, and rose without effort. The first and last rebellion of his life ended when his mother left. He still doesn't know whether his father truly accepted his fate, accepted being a "lucky one," or whether he insisted on going the opposite way only to convince himself that his earlier choice had been wrong, that his earlier rebellion had meant nothing.

His mother smoothed her skirt, sat down behind his castle, and bent her head to help him find the next block. Her hands were slender, her nails as red as poinsettia, her wrists bare. In no time she found the block he had been searching so hard for, the one that went on the pillar of the gatehouse, and handed it to him.

"Baby, the world is a big yarn ball, do you understand? However smooth and perfect the outside of the ball is, the inside can be completely different. It has no rules. Every stitch looks like it follows the rules to the letter, but really it can be knitted any which way. You can knit the ball in the most efficient way, or in the most elaborate, mysterious way. Time doesn't exist inside a yarn ball. Everything in your life, what time would call past and present and future, all exists at once. There's no before or after. There's just one possibility tangled up with another. You're only walking from one point of the yarn ball to another, moving from one possibility to the next." She paused, leaned over to stroke his hair, and went on. "If you're lucky enough, you'll get to walk to every point, and try every possibility."

Without thinking, he snapped off the poinsettia blooming in the pot on the windowsill and crushed it in his fingers. Sticky white sap coated his hand. With poinsettia, the bewitching red isn't the petals, it's the leaves. Poisonous, but not deadly.

"I thought I was lucky enough," he thought, crushing the last of the red leaves in his hand.

The world he had grown up in so far was a straight, plain narrative. He had no destiny, only a lot in life. Whatever he was born to be, he was, or rather he was on the road to becoming it. His beautiful, outrageous mother was nothing more than a trivial little episode in his family, in his life. When he saw the old Mexican man pushing an empanada cart through the bitter wind, the homeless man with a bed made of cardboard boxes, the girl sobbing in the shadow of a rock in Central Park with a big sketchboard in her arms, the junior employee in a cubicle agonizing over a promotion that wouldn't come, he always felt that whatever it was that swept other people's lives along and forced them forward, the sandstorm or the flood or the tornado, had never so much as touched the banks of his.

She found the blue blocks for the castle moat and handed them to him. He took the blue block and set it under the bridge; it was the last piece of the moat. He looked up at his mother, beaming with pride.

She smiled at him, touched his cheek, and went on softly: "Someday you'll get to the very deepest part of the yarn ball, and you'll feel a suffocation like weightlessness, and a nausea you can't name. You'll want to retch for a long time, you'll want to close your eyes and pretend to sleep to get away from it. You'll see some people swaggering, some scheming and scrabbling, some perfectly happy with themselves. You'll see the tallest building fall overnight, and the most passionate young man go white-haired overnight. You'll understand that the deepest love is also the heaviest chain, and the most painful parting can bring the sweetest anticipation. In the deepest night, you'll be blinded by glaring sunlight; on the brightest summer afternoon, you'll feel a cold that goes to the bone. You'll find that the homeless man who makes his home in a cardboard box also changes into a bright red sweatshirt he found, for Christmas. You'll find the world is full of contradictions, ridiculous, and there's nothing to be done about it. And most important, you'll find that the yarn ball doesn't begin at its deepest point, and doesn't end there either. Its two ends could be hidden anywhere in the ball."

She stood up. "So I'm saying, baby, I hope you'll walk to every point of this yarn ball. If you can walk to every point, only then will you be brave enough, and lucky enough."

That was the last thing he remembers his mother ever saying to him. And with that she put on her long red coat, opened the door, and left.

The door was suddenly pushed open, and the broker, all in grey, hurried in. He walked to the door, flicked away the poinsettia in his hand, his fingers itching, and shook the broker's hand. "So sorry, sir, I'm running late." The broker offered polite condolences on his father's death, then immediately broke into a huge smile.

"Could I have some time here by myself?" He cut off the broker's flood of words. "I've decided to buy it. Just email me the details, or we can set up a time to go over them." The broker's face lit up with an ecstasy that couldn't be hidden. A few pleasantries, and the broker left in a hurry, as if afraid he might change his mind at any moment.

He shut the door tight and sat down on the floor, in the spot where he had built with his blocks at five. His grandfather had fallen from power, his father had died, his lover and his friends had turned and walked away. His castle had collapsed before it was finished; at five he had lifted the half-built thing and smashed it. The blue moat, the pillars of the gate, he had no idea where they had been thrown. The castle he could never finish. The goodbye he never said.

"Partings and reunions should both have music," his father said to him before he died. "At the funeral, play 'The World We Knew.' It's the song we were listening to when your mother and I first met."

He took out his phone and found the battery was dead. His fingers itched and stung from the sap. In the dim room he finally couldn't hold it in any longer and began to cry. "Do I have to feel sick before I can walk the whole yarn ball? Do I have to walk all of it before I can be free? Do I have to be free before I can be lucky? Do I have to be lucky before you'll bless me?" His head was buried between his knees, his shoulders shaking uncontrollably. "If you bless me, will I not be killed by some sprig of mistletoe out of nowhere?"

The wind had died down. There was hardly anyone in the street, and everything was as silent as a boundless sea about to swallow his stranded boat. The neighborhood his father had avoided like the plague for so many years was as ordinary as ordinary can be. The towering plane trees, the red brick wall across the street, the gravel glinting under the streetlights. He smoothed the creases in his long black coat, dried the tears in his beautiful eyes, and left without looking back.
