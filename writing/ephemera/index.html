蜉蝣在世

蜉蝣在世

8 min read约 6 分钟First published on WeChat ↗首发于微信公众号 ↗

这是个温暖的夏日傍晚, 他坐在河边的长椅上看日落. 日落绚烂但又千篇一律 — 这是多么危险而诱人的组合, 后者让人厌倦和企划逃离, 前者又使人驻足欣赏和踌躇不决. 妻子刚刚发消息说明天就和儿子从罗马回来了, 她抱怨说带着儿子去了好多艺术馆和博物馆, 找专业人士全程私人讲解 — "毕竟艺术鉴赏能力要从小培养";但儿子无精打采, 什么也没听进去. 他看见消息, 才恍然间意识到已然是八月末尾. 又一个夏天要走了, 他不记得在纽约已经度过了多少个夏天.

成年之后人生中的里程碑无一例外的避开了夏天, 反而是冬天应有尽有、无微不至地提供了他稳定生活的所有 — 婚礼在隆冬举行, 儿子在冬末出生, 升职的消息也总在圣诞前夕被告知. 可纵使如此他仍然盼望夏天. 或许是因为这座城市的人们本就对夏天有着无与伦比的偏爱, 毕竟冬天太过冗长阴郁, 才让夏天显得弥足珍贵; 又或许是因为妻子总在夏天带着孩子离开纽约度长假, 他因工作难以脱身便一个人留下 — 宿醉无人过问, 晚归不需要解释, 河边漫步随心所欲. 这短暂而自由的夏天是他体面而一成不变的生活的唯一出口.

他自幼便记忆力超群 — 繁复的法律条文、绕口的德国客户名字、最爱的戏剧脚本里大段的人物独白, 支持的球队获胜赛季的比分, 他都可以一字不差地记下来. 可是唯独很多关于夏天的回忆他记得模模糊糊. 他仍然可以清晰的记得许多关于夏天的瞬间, 却难以一下子记起这些瞬间的归属地和归属时间以及它们之间的联系.他记得在72楼的楼顶天台俯身看这座城市的车水马龙, 为能短暂的远离身后的阿谀奉承和觥筹交错而庆幸, 吐出的烟圈还没来得及消散就湮灭在刚下过雨的潮湿空气中; 他记得在音乐的鼓点和灯光的明灭中, 依稀看见的那女孩手臂上红色的孔雀纹身; 他纳闷了很久, 这到底本来就是红色的孔雀, 还是被这红色镭射激光灯染了色? 他记得从昏暗烟雾缭绕的地下酒吧摇摇晃晃的出来, 正对面的废弃仓库墙面上画着猪头叼着烟卷的涂鸦, 烟蒂处的墙纸剥落凸起, 看起来像是即将掉落在地面的灰烬; 他清楚地记得一种味道, 那个味道像是把盛开的丁香花和烟草碎末尘封在木盒里多年再打开, 花香、檀木香、烟草香和灰尘混杂, 温暖而又陈旧.

人在回忆时是总是有一套自成体系的逻辑, 于他, 他的回忆起点总是味道. 那温暖而陈旧的味道属于那个孔雀纹身的女孩, 他从地下酒吧摇摇晃晃出来时, 那个女孩就站在废弃仓库的旁边抽烟; 而之所以去那个酒吧, 是因为在72楼天台举办的酒会早早结束. 他们第一次讲话, 是女孩开口问他:"嗨, 你怎么穿一身西装来这个地方?" 他还没来得及回答, 她皱了皱鼻子又说道:"小心喝这么多丢掉你的钻戒." 他连忙低下头看了看无名指的戒指, 不放心的摸了摸. 孔雀纹身的女孩叫Nae, 她在下城读书, 学音乐制作, 孔雀纹身确实是红色, 是她身上众多纹身的一个, 那个夏天她的头发从粉色染成金色, 又从金色染成火一般的红色.

他的大部分夏天是一个人度过, 但唯有那个夏天和Nae还有她的朋友们度过了很多时间. 她在Bushwick的几家夜店做客场DJ, 邀请他去看; 演出结束后她把他介绍给她的男友和她的朋友们, 他们要不是DJ, 要不是摄影师, 要不就是模特, 每个人走路都是介于要晕倒和优雅之间的轻飘飘. Nae总是这样介绍他:"这是我异父异母的哥哥, 为资本大鳄争取权益的精英律师. " 他尴尬笑笑, 习惯性地伸出手想要握手和接名片, 方觉不妥才赶快收回去. 她总是画奇奇怪怪的妆, 眼影的颜色和发色要么呼应要么相撞, 脸上贴亮晶晶的闪钻, 手上要戴至少五个戒指, 讲话充满讽刺但又不至于让人感到被冒犯; Nae的男友头发比她还长, 脖子被一条长长的龙纹身环绕, 涂黑色的指甲油, 笑起来舒展而飘飘然. 而他总是穿着衬衫和长裤, 肌肤苍白干净, 显得与他们格格不入.

他们住的很近, 演出结束后他开车载她回家. 车飞速地越过布鲁克林大桥, Nae总是让他加速 — '你开的再快一点我们没准可以摆脱地吸引力, 直接飞到月亮上. ' 他嘴上说着荒唐, 但脚下却悄悄踩了脚油门, 内心隐隐期待车开到桥顶时可以真的摆脱离心力. Nae从小跟着爸爸在塞浦路斯生活, 她爸爸是当地小有名气的建筑设计师, 给她在中央公园旁买了一套公寓. "离学校和布鲁克林太远了", Nae撇撇嘴, "我大部分时间都在布鲁克林, 曼哈顿属于你们这些精英". 她从小喜欢画画, 一直想当油画家; 后来来了纽约之后又喜欢上了电子音乐. "我觉得音乐是最能把人们团结在一起的东西, 尤其是Techno, 都不需要歌词, 只需要鼓点和节奏."Nae边说边连上车上的蓝牙, 放她自己录制的曲子. Nae的Spotify只有50个听众, 他成了她的第51个. Nae问他他的事, 他就只是简单的说说 — 妻子是大学同学, 恋爱5年, 结婚7年, 儿子刚刚5岁, 妻子带着儿子现在在马其它玩; 他毕业之后就在这家律所, 平时工作很忙, politics不少, 但好在自己资历深能力够. Nae睁大眼睛, 不可思议: "你真是模范公民; 我爸妈结婚的时间都没你们恋爱时间长." 他耸耸肩, 说:"我现在的生活很舒服; 你还太年轻了, 等过几年就明白了."Nae背靠在副驾驶的座椅上笑得喘不过气, 她说:"相信我, 再过二十年我也不会明白的."

他和妻子电话时提过Nae, 妻子满腹狐疑; 他再三解释他们只是忘年交, Nae的男友他都见过, 妻子还是不开心.末了他岔过话题, 再也没有和妻子说起过她的事情. 那个夏天很快结束, Nae秋季学期要和男友去佛罗伦萨交换, 和他匆匆地告了别就走了. 妻子和儿子也回了家, 儿子马上要上小学, 他们费尽心思地把儿子送进了上东区最好的私立学校. 他的工作也更忙了起来, 生活又渐渐地回到平淡的两点一线. 那年年底他又升职了, corporate ladder越爬越高. 妻子很是开心, 在太太聚会上总是装作不经意的提起, 然后满意地环顾四周捕捉众人眼底艳羡的神色.

"那是哪一年的夏天来着? " 天色已经暗沉, 落日的最后一抹余晖渐渐被水面淹没. "如果那年年底升职了的话, 应该是三年前吧." 他喃喃自语道. Nae回纽约之后偶尔联系过他, 他被工作和琐事牵绊也疏于回复. 然后Nae就毕业了, 毕业后就为了DJ梦又跑到了柏林, 夏天总是呆在Ibiza或是南法.

但他总是在之后的夏天想起Nae, 即使他们之后再没有度过一个夏天, 甚至都没有再相见. 他之前对Nae说过, 他觉得这个世界上是有两种人的, 一种人从出生起就知道自己的人生使命; 另一种人庸碌一生为生计奔波、从来没明白自己追求的是什么. Nae却不以为然, 她说这个世界上只有自由和不自由的人, 自由的人不被任何事情束缚, 甚至不被追求和使命束缚; 不自由的人会被任何事情束缚 — 社会规则, 他人目光, 甚至是小小的纹身, 都可以束缚他们看待世界的眼光.

他呆呆地盯着水面, 天色彻底的暗了下来, 拂面的风稍有凉意. 手里的Brooklyn Sour还剩一点, 他仰头喝下最后一口. 他知道明天过后, 就不可以再抽烟了, 他不能让家人吸二手烟. 也要少喝酒了, 毕竟伤害自己的身体. 他得更努力的工作, 妻子想再要一个孩子, 经济负担肯定会重一些.

他知道明天过后, 夏天就又结束了, 他又将再次回到他真实的生活中去. 他又将再次把Nae抛到脑后, 埋头到家庭琐事和工作当中去. 他将为妻子做的精致料理而幸福满足, 将为儿子的成长而骄傲开心, 将为自己升职或者make partner而长舒一口气. 他将忘了自己在夏天的快乐和痛苦. 他安慰自己道,毕竟蜉蝣在世, 又有谁是真正自由的呢.

It was a warm summer evening, and he was sitting on a bench by the river watching the sun go down. The sunset was spectacular and exactly like every other one, which is a dangerous, tempting combination: the sameness makes you restless and want to escape, and the spectacle makes you stop, and look, and stay. His wife had just texted that she and their son would be back from Rome tomorrow. She'd dragged him to so many galleries and museums, she complained, with a private guide every time, "because an eye for art has to start young," and their son had been bored stiff and taken in nothing. Reading her message, he realized with a start that it was already the end of August. Another summer leaving. He'd lost count of how many summers he'd spent in New York.

Since he'd grown up, every milestone in his life had steered clear of summer. Winter, on the other hand, had thoughtfully provided everything a stable life needs. The wedding was in the dead of winter, his son was born at the end of one, and every promotion was announced just before Christmas. Still, he waited all year for summer. Maybe because people in this city love summer beyond all reason, the winters being so long and grim. Or maybe because his wife always took their son out of New York for the summer while he stayed behind, alone, kept by work. No one asked about his hangovers. He didn't have to explain coming home late. He could walk by the river whenever he liked. Those short, free summers were the only way out of a life that was respectable and never changed.

He'd always had a remarkable memory. Tangled statutes, the tongue-twisting names of German clients, long monologues from his favorite plays, the scores from every season his team had won: he could recall all of it word for word. But his memories of summer, and only those, were blurred. He could still see certain summer moments clearly, but he struggled to place them, to say where and when they'd happened or how they connected. He remembered leaning over the edge of a rooftop seventy-two floors up, looking down at the traffic, glad to be away for a moment from the flattery and clinking glasses behind him, his smoke ring vanishing into the damp air after rain before it could even drift apart. He remembered a red peacock tattoo on a girl's arm, glimpsed through the beat and the strobing lights, and how he'd wondered for a long time afterward whether the peacock was really red or only colored by the red lasers. He remembered stumbling out of a dim, smoky basement bar and seeing, on the wall of the abandoned warehouse across the street, a graffiti pig with a cigarette in its mouth, the wallpaper peeling and curling at the tip so that it looked like ash about to fall. And he remembered a smell, clearly: like lilacs in bloom and loose tobacco sealed in a wooden box for years and then opened, flowers and sandalwood and tobacco and dust together, warm and old.

Everyone's memory has its own logic, and his always started with smell. That warm, old smell belonged to the girl with the peacock tattoo. When he'd stumbled out of the basement bar, she was standing by the warehouse smoking. And he'd only gone to that bar because the cocktail party on the seventy-second-floor roof had ended early. She spoke first. "Hey, why'd you wear a suit to a place like this?" Before he could answer she wrinkled her nose and added, "Careful you don't drink so much you lose your diamond ring." He looked down at his ring finger and touched the ring, uneasy. Her name was Nae. She studied music production at a school downtown. The peacock really was red, one of many tattoos, and that summer she dyed her hair from pink to blond and then from blond to a flaming red.

He spent most summers on his own, but that one summer he spent a lot of time with Nae and her friends. She played guest DJ sets at a few clubs in Bushwick and invited him along. Afterward she introduced him to her boyfriend and her friends, who were all DJs or photographers or models, and who all walked with the same floating lightness, somewhere between fainting and grace. Nae always introduced him the same way: "This is my brother from another mother, and another father. Hotshot lawyer, fights for the rights of the big capitalist sharks." He'd laugh awkwardly and reach out by habit to shake hands and swap business cards, then realize and pull his hand back. She always wore strange makeup, eyeshadow that either echoed or clashed with her hair, glitter gems on her cheeks, at least five rings, and her conversation was all irony that somehow never offended anyone. Her boyfriend's hair was longer than hers, a long dragon tattoo wound around his neck, he painted his nails black, and he had a loose, floating smile. And there he'd be in his shirt and slacks, his skin pale and clean, completely out of place.

They lived close to each other, so after her sets he drove her home. The car flew across the Brooklyn Bridge and Nae kept telling him to go faster. "A little faster and maybe we'll break free of gravity and fly straight to the moon." He told her that was ridiculous, and quietly pressed the gas, half hoping that at the top of the bridge they really would break free. Nae had grown up with her father in Cyprus. He was a well-known architect there, and he'd bought her an apartment by Central Park. "It's too far from school and from Brooklyn," she said, pulling a face. "I'm in Brooklyn most of the time. Manhattan's for you elites." She'd loved painting since she was little and always meant to be a painter, until she came to New York and fell for electronic music. "Music's the thing that brings people together most, I think. Especially techno. You don't even need words, just the beat." As she talked she connected to his car's Bluetooth and played him her own tracks. She had fifty listeners on Spotify, and he became the fifty-first. When she asked about him, he kept it simple. His wife was his college girlfriend. They'd dated five years and been married seven. Their son had just turned five. Right now his wife and son were in Malta. He'd been at the same law firm since graduating; it was busy, lots of politics, but he was senior enough and good enough. Nae's eyes went wide. "You're a model citizen. My parents weren't even married as long as you two dated." He shrugged. "My life's very comfortable. You're still young. Give it a few years and you'll understand." Nae threw herself back in the passenger seat, laughing so hard she could barely breathe. "Trust me. Give it twenty years and I still won't understand."

He mentioned Nae to his wife once on the phone, and she was immediately suspicious. He explained over and over that they were just friends, that the age difference was huge, that he'd met her boyfriend, but she was still unhappy, and in the end he changed the subject and never brought Nae up again. The summer ended quickly. Nae was going to Florence with her boyfriend for an exchange semester, and she said a hurried goodbye and left. His wife and son came home. Their son was starting school, and they'd gone to enormous lengths to get him into the best private school on the Upper East Side. Work got busier, and life slowly went back to the plain shuttle between home and office. At the end of that year he was promoted again, one more rung up the corporate ladder. His wife was delighted. At lunches with the other wives she always brought it up as if in passing, then glanced around, satisfied, catching the envy in everyone's eyes.

Which summer was that? The sky had darkened and the last of the sunset was sinking into the water. If that was the year I made the promotion, it must have been three years ago, he murmured to himself. After Nae came back to New York she'd been in touch now and then, but he was always caught up in work and errands and slow to reply. Then she graduated and went to Berlin to chase her DJ dream, and spent her summers in Ibiza or the south of France.

But every summer after that he thought of Nae, though they never spent another summer together, never even saw each other again. He'd told her once that he thought there were two kinds of people in the world: those who know their purpose from the day they're born, and those who spend their lives scraping by and never figure out what they want. Nae didn't agree. She said there were only free people and unfree people. Free people aren't bound by anything, not even by a purpose. Unfree people are bound by everything, society's rules, what other people think, even a little tattoo; anything can bind the way they see the world.

He stared at the water. It was fully dark now, and the breeze had a slight chill. There was a little Brooklyn Sour left in his glass, and he tipped his head back and finished it. After tomorrow, he knew, he couldn't smoke anymore; he couldn't have his family breathing it. He'd have to drink less too; it was bad for him. He'd have to work harder. His wife wanted another baby, and money would be tighter.

After tomorrow, he knew, summer would be over again, and he'd go back to his real life again. He'd put Nae out of his mind again and bury himself in work and family errands. He'd be happy and content over the beautiful meals his wife cooked, proud and pleased as his son grew, relieved when he was promoted or made partner. He'd forget his summer joy and his summer pain. We're all mayflies, he told himself. Who is ever really free?
